🔍 Diagnostics — Must-Know

Probing Depth (PD) vs Clinical Attachment Level (CAL)

CAL = PD + GM
GM = gingival margin position relative to CEJ.
• Recession (margin apical to CEJ): GM is positive → CAL > PD
• Overgrowth (margin coronal to CEJ): GM is negative → CAL < PD
CAL is the true measure of attachment loss — PD alone is misleading.

Probing Technique

  • Force: 0.20–0.25 N (≈ 20–25 g)
  • "Walking probe" — 1 mm steps along sulcus base
  • 6 sites/tooth: MB, B, DB, ML, L, DL
  • Probe types: WHO (ball tip), UNC-15, Williams, PCP-12
  • BOP within 10–30 s = active inflammation

CHAPTER 1

Anatomy, Structure & Function of the Periodontium

Maps to Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed. (2018), Chapter 2 — Anatomy, Structure and Function of the Periodontium.

DefinitionThe periodontium ("attachment apparatus" / supporting tissues of the tooth) comprises four distinct but functionally integrated components: gingiva, periodontal ligament (PDL), cementum, and alveolar bone. Its main biologic function is to attach the tooth to the bone of the jaws and maintain the integrity of the masticatory mucosa.

Oral mucosa zones

  • Masticatory gingiva + hard palate
  • Specialized dorsum of tongue
  • Lining remainder of oral cavity

Four components of periodontium

  • Gingiva — protective covering
  • Periodontal ligament — suspensory/sensory
  • Cementum — root surface mineralized tissue
  • Alveolar bone — bony socket

Gingiva

Anatomical Subdivisions

  • Marginal (free / unattached) gingiva — terminal edge surrounding teeth in a collar-like fashion; ~1 mm wide; forms the soft-tissue wall of the gingival sulcus. Separated from attached gingiva by the free gingival groove (visible in ~50% of cases). The most apical point of its contour is the gingival zenith.
  • Gingival sulcus — shallow V-shaped space between tooth and inner aspect of free gingiva. Ideal histologic depth ≈ 0 mm; mean histologic depth 1.8 mm (range 0–6); probing depth in clinically healthy gingiva = 2–3 mm (differs from histologic depth because of probe diameter, force, and inflammation).
  • Attached gingiva — firm, resilient, tightly bound to underlying periosteum of alveolar bone. Extends from free gingival groove to the mucogingival junction (MGJ). Widest in incisor region, narrower posteriorly. MGJ remains stable through adult life — width increases with age via coronal migration changes.
  • Interdental gingiva — fills the gingival embrasure under the contact area. May be pyramidal (anterior) or form a col (a vestibulo-lingual valley between facial and lingual papillae) in posterior regions. Shape depends on contact point, distance from contact to bone crest, and presence of recession; absent when a diastema exists.

Microscopic Composition

Stratified squamous epithelium overlying a fibrous connective tissue core (lamina propria). The principal cell is the keratinocyte; non-keratinocytes ("clear cells") include melanocytes, Langerhans cells (dendritic, antigen-presenting), and Merkel cells (tactile receptors).

Junctional Epithelium — Key Defense Features
  • Firmly fixed to tooth surface — forms epithelial seal against plaque
  • Allows passage of GCF, inflammatory cells, and immune defense components to the gingival margin
  • Rapid regeneration — maintains host-parasite equilibrium
  • Endocytic capacity equal to that of macrophages and neutrophils
  • Forms a functional dentogingival unit with the gingival fibers
  • JE is restored even after surgery/instrumentation and forms around implants

Keratinization Gradient (oral mucosa)

Palate (most keratinized) > gingiva > ventral tongue > cheek (least). Degree of gingival keratinization decreases with age and at menopause.

Gingival Connective Tissue (Lamina Propria)

  • Composition: ~60% collagen fibers, ~5% fibroblasts, ~35% ground substance + vessels/nerves
  • Two strata: papillary (between rete pegs) and reticular (continuous with periosteum)
  • Fibers: Type I collagen (tensile strength), Type IV (basement membrane / vessel walls), Type III (reticular). Elastic system = oxytalan, elaunin, elastin
  • Cellular elements: fibroblasts (dominant), mast cells, macrophages, occasional plasma cells, lymphocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, adipocytes

Gingival Fiber Groups

Dense bundles of Type I collagen that brace the marginal gingiva to the tooth, resist masticatory forces, and unite free gingiva with cementum and attached gingiva.

  • Gingivodental — from cement (below sulcus base) radiating coronally to crest and outer marginal gingiva surface
  • Circular — encircle the tooth as a ring within marginal and interdental gingiva
  • Transseptal — horizontal bundles connecting cement of adjacent teeth, above interdental bone crest; reform after periodontal bone loss
  • Semicircular — attach to one proximal surface, course around buccal/lingual margin, insert on opposite proximal surface of same tooth
  • Transgingival (Page) — diagonally cross interdental space linking proximal surfaces of multiple adjacent teeth

Continuous Tooth Eruption (Gottlieb–Orban concept)

  • Active eruption — tooth moves toward occlusal plane (compensates attrition)
  • Passive eruption — apical migration of gingiva exposing the tooth; now considered pathologic (chronic inflammation)
  • Four stages of passive eruption (1→4): JE migrates from enamel only → CEJ → onto cementum, exposing root surface
  • Distance from apical JE to alveolar crest remains constant at ~1.07 mm

Periodontal Ligament (PDL)

Highly cellular, vascular connective tissue (~0.2 mm wide) surrounding the root and uniting it to the alveolar bone wall. Width: narrowed in non-functional teeth, widened in hyperfunction; hourglass-shaped, narrowest at the axis of rotation (junction of apical and middle thirds of root in single-rooted teeth; interradicular bone in multi-rooted teeth).

Sharpey's Fibers

The terminal portions of principal fibers embedded into cementum and bundle bone. Heavily mineralized at insertion. Associated with osteopontin and bone sialoprotein, which regulate mineralization at high biomechanical sites.

Elastic System

No mature elastin; only immature forms — oxytalan (vertical, attached to cement in cervical third, may regulate vascular flow) and elaunin.

Cellular Components of PDL

Connective tissue cells

  • Fibroblasts (most abundant) — synthesize & phagocytose collagen
  • Osteoblasts / osteoclasts
  • Cementoblasts / odontoclasts
  • Undifferentiated mesenchymal stem cells

Other cells

  • Epithelial rests of Malassez — remnants of Hertwig's root sheath; abundant cervically and apically; may calcify into cementicles; involved in cyst formation and cementum repair
  • Immune/defense cells (neutrophils, lymphocytes, macrophages, mast cells, eosinophils)
  • Neurovascular elements

Functions of the PDL

Mechanical / Supportive

  • Attach tooth to bone
  • Transmit occlusal forces to bone
  • Shock absorption (viscoelastic + tensional theories)
  • Soft-tissue casing for vessels and nerves
  • Maintain gingiva–tooth relationship

Formative & Remodeling

  • Cells participate in apposition/resorption of cementum and bone
  • Collagen turnover 2× gingiva, 4× skin
  • Continuous remodeling under occlusal/orthodontic forces

Nutritive

  • Supplies cementum, bone, gingiva via blood vessels
  • Highly vascular (~10% volume in rodents) — hydrodynamic cushioning
  • Lymphatic drainage

Sensory

  • Tactile, pressure, pain — via trigeminal pathways
  • Four nerve endings: free (pain), Ruffini (apical mechanoreceptor), Meissner (mid-root), encapsulated spindle (apex, pressure/vibration)
Shock-absorption theories Tensional — principal fibers unfold/straighten and transmit force, elastically deforming the bony socket. Viscoelastic — extracellular fluid is displaced through cribriform plate foramina (Volkmann canals) into marrow spaces first; fibers tense only after fluid depletion.

Cementum

Avascular, mineralized mesenchymal tissue covering the anatomic root. Inorganic content ≈ 45–50% hydroxyapatite (lower than bone 65%, dentin 70%, enamel 97%). Matrix = mainly Type I collagen (~90%) + Type III (~5%) + non-collagenous proteins (proteoglycans, osteopontin, bone sialoprotein).

Schroeder's Classification (5 types)

  • Acellular afibrillar cementum (AAC) — only mineralized ground substance; coronal cementum; 1–15 μm
  • Acellular extrinsic fiber cementum (AEFC) — almost entirely Sharpey's fibers; cervical third; produced by fibroblasts + cementoblasts; 30–230 μm
  • Cellular mixed stratified cementum (CMSC) — extrinsic + intrinsic fibers, may have cells; apical third and furcations; 100–1000 μm
  • Cellular intrinsic fiber cementum (CIFC) — fills resorption lacunae; cementoblast-derived; no extrinsic fibers
  • Intermediate cementum — at cementodentinal junction; entraps Hertwig sheath cell rests

Cementoenamel Junction (CEJ) Relationships

Overlap (60–90%)

Cementum overlaps enamel — most common.

Edge-to-edge (~30%)

Cementum meets enamel without overlap.

Gap (5–10%)

Cementum and enamel do not meet — exposed dentin → hypersensitivity with recession.

Cementum thickness, resorption & repair

  • Thickness triples between ages 11 and 70 (mean 95 μm at 20 → 215 μm at 60)
  • Greatest in apical third and furcations; greater distally than mesially (mesial drift)
  • Hypercementosis = excessive thickening (age-related, may complicate extraction)
  • Permanent teeth do not undergo physiologic resorption (unlike deciduous); however, ~70% of resorption sites limited to cementum without dentin involvement
  • Resorption causes: trauma from occlusion, orthodontics, cysts, tumors, replantation, periapical and periodontal disease, systemic factors (Ca deficiency, hypothyroidism, Paget disease)
  • Repair needs viable CT (not epithelium); reversal line demarcates new cementum
  • Ankylosis — fusion of cementum to bone with PDL obliteration; abnormal repair after resorption

Alveolar Bone

The portion of maxilla and mandible that forms and supports tooth sockets. Tooth-dependent: develops with eruption, resorbs after tooth loss. Composition: 2/3 inorganic (hydroxyapatite + Ca/P/Mg/F/citrate/carbonate ions) and 1/3 organic matrix (Type I collagen 90% + osteocalcin, BMPs, osteopontin, bone sialoprotein, proteoglycans).

Anatomic divisions

Alveolar bone proper

Thin compact inner wall lining the socket. Contains the cribriform plate — perforations through which neurovascular bundles connect PDL with cancellous bone. Appears as lamina dura radiographically.

Bundle bone

Specialized alveolar bone proper adjacent to PDL containing many Sharpey's fibers. Thin parallel lamellae, intercalated apposition lines. Provides fiber anchorage.

Supporting bone

Cortical plates (haversian + compact lamellae) on facial and lingual surfaces + cancellous (spongy) trabeculae between cribriform plate and cortical plates. Predominates interradicularly and interdentally.

Interdental septum

  • Spongy bone bordered by cribriform plates of adjacent teeth + facial/lingual cortical plates
  • If interdental space is narrow → may consist only of cribriform plate; if roots too close → "fenestration window"
  • Crest of septum parallels a line connecting adjacent CEJs
  • Crest–CEJ distance: 0.75–1.49 mm in young adults (mean 1.08 mm); increases with age/disease (to ~2.81 mm)

Bone topography & defects

Fenestration

Isolated denuded area on root with intact marginal bone; root covered only by periosteum and gingiva.

Dehiscence

Denuded area extending through the marginal bone. Occurs in ~20% of teeth; more frequent facially, on anterior teeth, often bilateral; predisposing factors: prominent roots, malposition, thin labial plates.

Cellular components

  • Osteoblasts — produce organic matrix (osteoid) which mineralizes
  • Osteocytes — entrapped osteoblasts within lacunae, communicating via canaliculi
  • Osteoclasts — multinucleated, hematopoietic origin; resorb bone via ruffled border secreting acid + hydrolytic enzymes (creating Howship's lacunae)
  • Periosteum — outer fibrous layer + inner osteogenic layer; endosteum lines marrow spaces (single osteoblast layer)

Bone remodeling

Alveolar bone is the least stable periodontal tissue — continuously remodeled by coupled osteoblastic apposition and osteoclastic resorption. Influences include local (occlusal demand) and systemic (PTH, calcitonin, vitamin D) factors. Tension stimulates osteoblastic activity; compression promotes osteoclastic resorption — the basis of orthodontic tooth movement and mesial drift.

Trauma from occlusion Forces exceeding the adaptive capacity of the periodontium produce a histologic lesion (trauma from occlusion). Clinically inferred from radiographic + clinical indicators (e.g., widened PDL space, mobility).
Disuse atrophy Reduced function → fewer/thinner trabeculae, PDL atrophy with fibers reduced, disoriented, and finally parallel to root surface.

Blood Supply, Lymphatics & Innervation

Gingival blood supply — three sources

  • Supraperiosteal arterioles along facial and lingual alveolar bone surfaces — capillaries to sulcular epithelium and outer gingival rete network
  • PDL vessels extending into gingiva and anastomosing with sulcus capillaries
  • Interdental septal arterioles emerging through crestal bone, anastomosing with PDL and crevicular capillaries

PDL blood supply — three sources

  • Apical vessels (branches before entering pulp)
  • Transalveolar/perforating vessels through cribriform plate
  • Anastomotic vessels from gingiva (cervical region)

Lymphatic drainage

Begins in CT papillae → external collecting network on periosteum → regional nodes, primarily submandibular group. Lymphatics beneath the JE pass into PDL alongside blood vessels.

Innervation

  • Gingiva: labial, buccal, palatine nerves + PDL fibers (trigeminal V)
  • Sensory endings: Meissner corpuscles (tactile), Krause end-bulbs (thermal), encapsulated spindle endings
  • PDL: free nerve endings (pain), Ruffini (apical mechanoreceptor), Meissner-like (mid-root), encapsulated spindle (apex, pressure/vibration)

Development of the Attachment Apparatus

  • Hertwig's epithelial root sheath — apical extension of reduced enamel epithelium; determines root form; fragments → epithelial rests of Malassez
  • Cementogenesis — sheath breakdown allows dental follicle mesenchymal cells to contact dentin and differentiate into cementoblasts → secrete cementoid → mineralizes
  • PDL formation — follicle fibroblasts produce collagen, fibers acquire oblique orientation; first bundles near CEJ form gingivodental fibers; transseptal & alveolar crest fibers appear at eruption
  • Alveolar bone — develops around each follicle by intramembranous ossification; fuses with basal bone (both derive from neural-crest ectomesenchyme). Resorbs after tooth loss
  • JE formation — at eruption, reduced enamel epithelium fuses with oral epithelium and is gradually transformed into the JE (1–2 years), maintaining attachment to the tooth throughout
Key clinical take-aways
  • The periodontium is a dynamic, tooth-dependent unit — loss of the tooth leads to loss of its supporting bone.
  • The dentogingival unit (JE + gingival fibers) is the first defense against subgingival bacterial colonization.
  • CEJ–crest distance and the integrity of the cribriform plate are key radiographic landmarks for diagnosing periodontal bone loss.
  • Knowledge of CEJ variants is essential for scaling/root planing and managing root sensitivity.
  • Fiber orientation explains why oblique fibers absorb most masticatory load and why transseptal fibers reorganize even after bone destruction.

Derived from reduced enamel epithelium; non-keratinized stratified squamous; attached to tooth via internal basal lamina and hemidesmosomes; very high turnover (4-6 days); permeable barrier allowing GCF flow.

Alveolar crest, horizontal, oblique (most numerous), apical, and interradicular fibers. Plus gingival fiber groups: dentogingival, dentoperiosteal, circular, transseptal.

Cementum overlaps enamel (~60-65%); cementum meets enamel edge-to-edge (~30%); gap with exposed dentin (~5-10%) — predisposes to hypersensitivity.

Attached gingiva: keratinized, pink, stippled, firmly bound to bone, ends at mucogingival junction. Alveolar mucosa: non-keratinized, red, loose, mobile, with visible vessels.

Q9

What is the col and why is it clinically important?

A9

A valley-shaped, non-keratinized interdental gingival depression beneath the contact point connecting facial and lingual papillae. Its thin non-keratinized epithelium makes it the most susceptible site for plaque-induced inflammation.

Q10

A10

Fibroblasts (most numerous, collagen synthesis/turnover); cementoblasts; osteoblasts/osteoclasts; epithelial rests of Malassez (remnants of Hertwig's sheath); undifferentiated mesenchymal cells; defense cells (macrophages, mast).

Q11

What is the blood supply to the periodontium?

A11

Supraperiosteal arterioles (gingiva), vessels from PDL, and vessels from interdental/interradicular alveolar bone. Three sources anastomose in the gingiva.

Q12

List the main functions of the periodontal ligament.

A12

Supportive (suspends tooth in socket), sensory (proprioception/pain via mechanoreceptors), nutritive (vessels supply cementum/bone), formative (cementoblasts, osteoblasts, fibroblasts), and remodeling/resorptive.

Biofilm & Periodontal Microbiology

Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed., 2018 - Chapter 8: Biofilm and Periodontal Microbiology

The Oral Cavity as a Microbial Ecosystem

The oral cavity is an open growth system with continuous ingestion and removal of microorganisms. Microbes survive by adhering to soft tissues or hard surfaces; otherwise they are cleared by swallowing, mastication, oral hygiene, salivary/crevicular flushing, and ciliary motility.

Six Major Oral Ecosystems (Niches)
  • Supragingival hard surfaces (teeth, implants, restorations, prostheses)
  • Subgingival region adjacent to hard surfaces (periodontal/peri-implant pocket)
  • Vestibular, palatal, and floor-of-mouth epithelium
  • Dorsum of the tongue
  • Tonsils
  • Saliva

Teeth and implants are microbiologically unique: (1) they provide a non-shedding hard surface allowing extensive structured bacterial deposits, and (2) they create a unique interruption of the ectoderm sealed by the junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment.

The Biofilm Concept

A biofilm is a structured community of microbial cells encased in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids), adherent to a surface or interface.

Key Biofilm Properties

  • Microcolonies separated by water channels that act as a primitive circulatory system, delivering nutrients and removing waste
  • Matrix acts as a barrier, concentrates bacterial products, and maintains metabolic interactions
  • Bacteria exist in an attached (sessile) state distinct from the free-floating planktonic state
  • Facilitates cell-to-cell signaling and horizontal gene transfer (including antibiotic resistance)
  • 1 g of plaque (wet weight) contains ~1011 bacteria; >750 microbial phylotypes identified
Clinical RelevanceBacteria in biofilms are 1000-1500 times more resistant to antibiotics than planktonic forms. Mechanical disruption is therefore essential — antimicrobials alone cannot eliminate plaque.

Stages of Biofilm Formation

1. Acquired Pellicle

Within minutes of cleaning, a film of >180 salivary peptides/glycoproteins (mucins, proline-rich proteins, statherins, histatins, keratins) coats the enamel. Provides adhesion receptors for bacteria; many proteins retain enzymatic activity.

2. Initial Adhesion

Non-specific transport followed by specific adhesin-receptor binding. Within 4-8 hours, Streptococcus spp. dominate (>20%). Other early colonizers: Actinomyces, Veillonella, Haemophilus, Neisseria.

3. Colonization / Co-adhesion

Primary colonizers provide new attachment sites for secondary colonizers via co-adhesion. F. nucleatum is a key "bridging" organism, coaggregating with virtually all oral bacteria.

4. Maturation

Shift from Gram-positive cocci/rods to Gram-negative rods, filaments, and spirochetes. Anaerobic, asaccharolytic species (using amino acids/peptides) dominate the mature plaque.

Quorum Sensing & Intercellular Communication

Quorum SensingBacteria secrete signaling molecules (autoinducers) that accumulate locally. Once a threshold concentration is reached (the "quorum"), gene expression changes occur. Roles include: modulating antibiotic-resistance gene expression, promoting growth of cooperative species, suppressing competitors, and regulating virulence factor production.

  • Epithelial cell invasion (intracellular reservoir)
  • IgA/IgG protease secretion

Etiologic Hypotheses

Non-specific Plaque Hypothesis

Disease results from total plaque accumulation overwhelming host defenses; any bacteria, given sufficient quantity, cause disease. Contradicted by: heavy plaque without periodontitis in some patients; site specificity of disease.

Specific Plaque Hypothesis

Only certain microorganisms are pathogenic regardless of total plaque mass. Reinforced by recognition of A. actinomycetemcomitans in localized aggressive periodontitis. Limitation: pathogens may exist without disease and vice versa.

Ecological Plaque Hypothesis

Both plaque quantity and composition matter. Environmental shifts (inflammation, increased GCF, smoking, hormones) disrupt microbial homeostasis, allowing minor pathogenic species to overgrow — a state called dysbiosis.

Keystone Pathogen / PSD Model

Certain low-abundance pathogens (e.g. P. gingivalis) orchestrate disease by remodeling a benign microbiota into a dysbiotic one. The Polymicrobial Synergy & Dysbiosis (PSD) model emphasizes interaction between keystone and accessory pathogens.

Antibiotic Resistance in Biofilms

Mechanisms of Biofilm Resistance (1000-1500x higher than planktonic)
  • Diffusion barrier: Matrix limits penetration of antimicrobials
  • Enzymatic inactivation: Extracellular β-lactamases, formaldehyde-lyase, formaldehyde-dehydrogenase trapped & concentrated in matrix (macrolides unaffected)
  • Multidrug efflux pumps: "Super-resistant" cells expel antibiotics
  • Altered metabolism: Slow-growing/dormant "persister" cells tolerate antibiotics targeting growth processes
  • Horizontal gene transfer: Dense cell packing facilitates plasmid & transposon exchange between species and even genera
  • Quorum-sensing modulation: Coordinated upregulation of resistance genes

Microbial Shifts: Health → Disease

Five Classical Shifts in Transition to Periodontitis
  • Gram-positive → Gram-negative
  • Cocci → rods → spirochetes
  • Non-motile → motile
  • Facultative anaerobes → obligate anaerobes
  • Fermentative (saccharolytic) → proteolytic species

Disease initiation requires three concurrent factors: (1) a susceptible host, (2) presence of pathogenic species at sufficient levels, and (3) absence or reduced proportion of beneficial species (e.g. S. sanguinis, which produces H2O2 that kills A. actinomycetemcomitans).

1. Overview of the 2017 World Workshop Classification

A classification of periodontal diseases is necessary for clinicians (to diagnose and treat patients correctly) and for researchers (to investigate etiology, pathogenesis, natural history, and treatment). For two decades the 1999 International Workshop classification (IWC1999) was used worldwide, but it accumulated limitations: imprecise case definitions, substantial overlap between categories (especially chronic vs aggressive periodontitis), and a one-dimensional severity-based approach.

The 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions (WWC2017), co-sponsored by the AAP and EFP, became operational in June 2018. It introduced a multidimensional staging and grading framework analogous to oncologic TNM staging.

Three main domains of WWC2017:
  1. Periodontal health, gingival diseases and conditions
  2. Periodontitis (necrotizing; as manifestation of systemic disease; periodontitis with stages I–IV and grades A–C)
  3. Other conditions affecting the periodontium (systemic, abscesses/endo-perio, mucogingival, occlusal trauma, tooth/prosthesis-related factors) and peri-implant diseases

2. Periodontal Health, Gingival Diseases & Conditions

A gingivitis patient can return to health, but a periodontitis patient remains a periodontitis patient for life and requires lifelong supportive therapy.

3.2 Case Definition of Periodontitis

A patient is a "case of periodontitis" when there is:
  • Interdental CAL detectable at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth, OR
  • Buccal/oral CAL ≥3 mm with pockets >3 mm detectable at ≥2 teeth.
The CAL cannot be attributable to non-periodontal causes: traumatic recession, cervical caries, CAL distal to a 2nd molar from 3rd molar malposition/extraction, draining endodontic lesion, vertical root fracture.
Why CAL replaced probing depth as the case-defining variable. Probing depth confounds pocket formation (apical migration of junctional epithelium) with gingival overgrowth or recession. CAL, measured from the cementoenamel junction, captures the true cumulative attachment lost over the life of the tooth — independent of the position of the gingival margin. A patient with 8 mm of recession and a 2 mm pocket has 10 mm of CAL and severe disease, even though "the pocket" appears shallow. Conversely, gingival enlargement may yield a 6 mm "pocket" with zero CAL — not periodontitis. WWC2017 therefore defines the case on interdental CAL, and uses radiographic bone loss only when CAL is not available.

3.3 Necrotizing Periodontal Diseases

Three forms on a spectrum of severity/host compromise:

  • Necrotizing gingivitis (NG / NUG) — papillary necrosis, ulceration, yellow-grey pseudomembrane, spontaneous bleeding, pain, fetid breath. Triggers: psychological stress, smoking, immunosuppression, malnutrition. Responds well to antimicrobial therapy + mechanical debridement.
  • Necrotizing periodontitis (NP / NUP) — adds clinical attachment loss and rapid alveolar bone destruction from interproximal regions. Almost exclusively in immunocompromised patients (HIV+ with low CD4).
  • Necrotizing stomatitis — necrosis extends beyond the periodontium into adjacent oral tissues (e.g., hard palate); life-threatening in severely compromised hosts.

3.7 Three-Step Staging & Grading Algorithm (AAP, 2018)

The AAP's practical workflow distills the whole framework into three sequential steps. Use it at chairside on every new periodontitis patient.

Step 1 — Initial case overview
  • Full-mouth probing depths
  • Full-mouth radiographs (with RBL assessment)
  • Count of missing teeth (and reason for loss)
Triage: mild–moderate destruction usually maps to Stage I or Stage II; severe–very severe to Stage III or Stage IV.
Step 2 — Establish the stage For suspected Stage I/II:
  • Confirm interdental CAL
  • Rule out non-perio causes of CAL (cervical caries/restorations, root fracture, trauma)
  • Determine maximum CAL or RBL
  • Confirm pattern of bone loss (horizontal vs vertical)
For suspected Stage III/IV add:
  • Tooth loss due to periodontitis (lost + planned for extraction)
  • Complexity factors: PD ≥6 mm, vertical defect ≥3 mm, furcation II/III, masticatory dysfunction, bite collapse, <20 teeth
Step 3 — Establish the grade
  • Calculate % RBL ÷ age at the worst tooth (<0.25 A, 0.25–1.0 B, >1.0 C)
  • Apply risk modifiers — smoking (cig/day), diabetes (HbA1c)
  • Measure response to SRP & plaque control
  • Estimate expected future bone loss rate
  • Account for systemic inflammatory burden (hsCRP) and overall medical status
Detailed step-by-step decision tree (EFP, Sanz & Tonetti 2019)

Step A — New patient triage. Panoramic X-ray with RBL? → suspected periodontitis. No RBL → check interdental CAL. No CAL → check for buccal recession with PD >3 mm. None → assess BOP: ≥10% sites → gingivitis; <10% → periodontal health.

Step B — Confirm the case. Detectable interdental CAL not attributable to local non-periodontal causes, at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth → perform full periodontal examination (charting, full-mouth radiographs).

Step C — Extent. <30% of teeth involved → localized; ≥30% → generalized; first molars + incisors only → molar-incisor pattern.

Step D — Stage III/IV vs I/II. CAL ≥5 mm or RBL ≥1/3 mid-root at ≥2 adjacent teeth → III or IV. Furcation Class II/III → III or IV. PD >5 mm at ≥2 adjacent teeth → III or IV. Tooth loss from periodontitis → III or IV. Otherwise → I or II.

Step E — Stage I vs II. RBL <15% & CAL 1–2 mm → Stage I. RBL 15–33% & CAL 3–4 mm → Stage II.

Step F — Stage III vs IV. ≤4 teeth lost, ≥10 opposing pairs, no bite collapse → III. ≥5 teeth lost, <10 pairs, bite collapse / drifting / severe ridge defect → IV.

Step G — Grade. Use longitudinal data if available (no loss = A; <2 mm in 5 yr = B; ≥2 mm in 5 yr = C). Otherwise use % RBL/age (<0.25 = A; 0.25–1.0 = B; >1.0 = C). Then apply smoking and HbA1c modifiers.

3.8 Case Examples — Worked Stage/Grade Diagnoses

Case 1 — Generalized Stage III, Grade C (diabetes-modified)
58-year-old smoker (15 cig/day), HbA1c 8.4%, type 2 diabetes.
Findings: generalized interdental CAL 5–7 mm; RBL extending to the middle third on multiple teeth; one furcation Class II on #36; max PD 7 mm; no tooth loss from perio; ≥30% of teeth involved.
→ Stage III (CAL ≥5 mm + furcation II), generalized.
→ Start at Grade B; HbA1c ≥7% and ≥10 cig/day push to Grade C.
Diagnosis: Generalized periodontitis, Stage III, Grade C.
Case 2 — Localized Stage II, Grade A
45-year-old non-smoker, healthy, no diabetes.
Findings: CAL 3–4 mm at five non-adjacent posterior teeth (about 20% of dentition); horizontal RBL 20–25%; max PD 5 mm; no furcation involvement; no tooth loss.
→ CAL 3–4 mm + RBL coronal third (15–33%) → Stage II; <30% of teeth → localized.
→ % RBL/age ≈ 22/45 = 0.49 (Grade B) — but heavy plaque with destruction commensurate to deposits, no risk factors → clinician may judge as Grade A.
Diagnosis: Localized periodontitis, Stage II, Grade A–B.
Case 3 — Generalized Stage IV, Grade C
62-year-old, prior loss of 3 teeth from periodontitis, 2 more hopeless and scheduled for extraction (total = 5). Fewer than 20 remaining teeth, mobility grade 2 on multiple anterior teeth with flaring of upper incisors, posterior bite collapse, complex prosthetic rehab required.
→ ≥5 teeth lost to perio + masticatory dysfunction + bite collapse + <20 teeth → Stage IV, generalized.
→ % RBL/age >1.0 with rapid destruction phenotype → Grade C.
Diagnosis: Generalized periodontitis, Stage IV, Grade C.
Case 4 — Molar-incisor pattern, Stage III, Grade C (early onset)
22-year-old non-smoker, no systemic disease, minimal plaque.
Findings: deep pockets (7–9 mm) and angular bone defects confined to first molars and incisors; CAL 6 mm at #16, #26, #36, #46 and central incisors; destruction far exceeds biofilm load.
→ CAL ≥5 mm + vertical defects → Stage III; only first molars and incisors involved → molar-incisor distribution.
→ % RBL/age >1.0 with destruction exceeding plaque, early onset → Grade C.
Diagnosis: Periodontitis, molar-incisor pattern, Stage III, Grade C.
Case 5 — Stage III → Stage II after regeneration (the only legitimate downgrade)
Originally diagnosed as Stage III because of an isolated 4 mm vertical defect with Class II furcation involvement on #46. After successful regenerative surgery: CAL stabilized at 3–4 mm throughout the dentition, furcation now Class I/not detectable, all PD ≤5 mm.
→ Per the AAP FAQ, this is the one permitted scenario where the stage may move down — from Stage III to Stage II. Outside successful regeneration the stage is fixed for life.
Case 6 — Recurrent unstable case (stage upgrade)
Patient previously diagnosed Stage II, Grade B, treated and clinically stable. During SPT, two new sites show CAL progression and a new Class II furcation appears on #17; two additional teeth become unsalvageable and are extracted because of perio.
→ Development of furcation II + tooth loss from perio → stage upgraded to Stage III. This is now classified as recurrent, unstable periodontitis, not "new" periodontitis.

3.9 Common FAQs on the 2018 Classification

Q: What is the biggest practical difference vs the 1999 classification?
A: The merging of "chronic" and "aggressive" into a single "periodontitis" category — no evidence of distinct pathophysiology was found — together with the move from a one-dimensional severity diagnosis to a multidimensional stage (severity + tooth loss + complexity) and grade (rate of progression + risk factors + systemic impact).
Q: Do stage and grade make the diagnosis?
A: No. The diagnosis of periodontitis is established first by the case definition (interdental CAL at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth). Stage and grade are then layered on top to describe extent, severity, complexity, expected rate of progression, treatment difficulty, and systemic impact.
Q: Can I assign a different stage per quadrant or sextant?
A: No. Stage is a whole-mouth diagnosis. If a patient has generalized mild–moderate destruction plus localized severe destruction, the worst-affected sites set the stage — e.g., the patient becomes Generalized Stage III (or IV if ≥5 teeth are lost to perio).
Q: Does the most severe site decide the stage?
A: Yes — the staging system is deliberately driven by the patient's most destroyed area, because that area dictates complexity of management and prognosis.
Q: How do I count "tooth loss due to periodontitis"?
A: It includes teeth already extracted for periodontal reasons and teeth currently planned for extraction as part of active periodontal therapy. Teeth lost to caries, trauma, or orthodontics do not count.
Q: Can the stage move down after treatment?
A: Generally no — once Stage III or IV, always Stage III or IV. The single exception: a Stage III case based on a vertical defect ≥3 mm or Class II furcation that has been successfully regenerated (CAL 3–4 mm, furcation Class I or undetectable, PD ≤5 mm) may move down to Stage II. Stages can move up if the case becomes unstable.
Q: How many grades does a patient get?
A: One. Start at Grade B, then shift based on direct or indirect evidence of progression, and apply smoking and diabetes modifiers using clinical judgment.
Q: Will my insurance reimbursement change because of Stage II vs Stage III?
A: No. Per the AAP, third-party payers do not reimburse based on disease severity; they reimburse based on the documentation supporting the procedure performed (probings, radiographs, etc.). The classification influences ICD-10 diagnosis codes but, currently, those are not required for most dental insurance claims.
Q: How does grading change the treatment plan?
A: Grade C automatically pulls behavioral and medical interventions into the plan: documented smoking-cessation counselling, referral to the physician for tighter glycemic control if HbA1c >7%, and shorter SPT intervals. Compliance with these modifiers — not just mechanical therapy — drives the long-term prognosis.

4. Other Conditions Affecting the Periodontium

4.1 Periodontal Abscesses & Endo-Periodontal Lesions

  • Periodontal abscess — localized purulent infection of periodontal tissues, classified by tissue of origin (gingival, periodontal, pericoronal).
  • Endo-periodontal lesions — pulpal necrosis precedes periodontal change; infection extends through PDL via accessory canals or apex.
  • Perio-endo lesions — periodontal pocket infection reaches pulp via lateral canals or apex, causing pulp necrosis.
  • Combined (true) lesions — independent periapical and periodontal lesions converge. Endodontic treatment must be completed before definitive periodontal therapy, especially before regenerative procedures.

4.2 Mucogingival Conditions & Deformities

The 2017 classification introduced new case definitions linked to treatment of gingival recession, integrating gingival phenotype (the new term replacing "biotype"), keratinized tissue width, root surface characteristics, and CEJ visibility.

  • Gingival recession (buccal/oral; interproximal/papillary)
  • Lack of keratinized gingiva
  • Decreased vestibular depth
  • Aberrant frenum / muscle attachment
  • Gingival excess: false pockets, irregular margin, hyperplasia, drug-induced enlargement
  • Abnormal pigmentation
  • Conditions of the exposed root surface

4.3 Traumatic Occlusal Forces

The new term "traumatic occlusal force" replaces "excessive occlusal force" — defined as any force exceeding the adaptive capacity of the periodontium and/or teeth.

  • Primary occlusal trauma — excessive forces on a tooth with normal periodontal support
  • Secondary occlusal trauma — normal/excessive forces on a tooth with reduced support
  • Orthodontic forces — now recognized as potentially traumatic

Current human evidence does not support traumatic occlusal forces as a driver of attachment loss progression in periodontitis — but they may worsen recession and non-carious cervical lesions.

4.4 Tooth- and Prosthesis-Related Factors

The term "supracrestal attached tissue" replaces the older "biologic width." Indirect restorative procedures themselves (impression, retraction, temporization) can cause recession and CAL.

  • Local tooth factors: cervical enamel projections, enamel pearls, palatogingival grooves (incisors — 8.5% prevalence), proximal root grooves, root fractures, cervical root resorption, cemental tears
  • Restoration-related factors: margins violating supracrestal tissues, overhangs, poor contour, materials, retentive surfaces

4.5 Peri-Implant Diseases & Conditions

ConditionKey features
Peri-implant healthAbsence of visible inflammation and BOP. Compatible with normal or reduced bone support. No defined PD range — increased PD is not by itself incompatible with health.
Peri-implant mucositisBOP plus visible inflammation; no progressive bone loss. Plaque-induced and reversible with plaque control.
Peri-implantitisBOP / suppuration, increased PD, progressive marginal bone loss beyond initial remodeling. Plaque-associated; non-linear, accelerating progression if untreated. Higher risk in patients with history of severe periodontitis.

Final diagnostic statement — three dimensions:
  1. Case definition — detectable interdental CAL at ≥2 non-adjacent teeth
  2. Form of periodontitis — necrotizing / manifestation of systemic disease / periodontitis
  3. Stage and grade, with extent/distribution descriptor — e.g., "Generalized periodontitis, Stage III, Grade B, modified to Grade C by poorly-controlled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 8.4%)."
Once written down, the stage and grade become the "guiding stars" for treatment difficulty, prognosis, and maintenance expectations for the life of the patient.

Basic Concepts of Immunity & Inflammation

Reference: Nitin Saroch. Periobasics: A Textbook of Periodontics and Implantology, 1st ed., Sushrut Publications, 2017 — Chapter 6: Basic Concepts in Immunity and Inflammation.

Immunity is the resistance of the organism to disease, especially infectious disease. The immune system is a collection of cells, tissues and molecules whose role is to prevent or limit infection. When dysregulated, the response itself becomes harmful, producing autoimmune disorders (rheumatic fever, asthma, glomerulonephritis, multiple sclerosis, desquamative gingivitis, etc.).

2. Innate Immunity

  • Mucus, saliva, gingival crevicular fluid

  • Low pH, lysozyme, lactoferrin

  • Alternative — triggered directly by microbial surfaces (LPS)
  • Lectin — mannose-binding lectin recognizes microbial carbohydrates

Functions: opsonization (C3b), chemotaxis (C5a), anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a), MAC (C5b–C9) for cell lysis.

  • VH/VL bind antigen; Fc mediates effector functions

Cell-mediated — T cells

T lymphocytes mature in the thymus and recognize antigen only when presented on MHC molecules via TCR.

  • CD4 helper — recognize MHC II on APCs
  • CD8 cytotoxic — recognize MHC I; kill virus-infected and tumor cells
  • Normal T:B ratio ≈ 3:1

7. Vascular & Cellular Events of Acute Inflammation

Vascular phase

  • Transient vasoconstriction → sustained vasodilation (histamine, NO) → erythema, heat
  • Increased vascular permeability → plasma protein leak → exudate
  • Hemoconcentration & stasis of blood flow

Cellular phase — neutrophil transendothelial migration (diapedesis)

  1. Margination — leukocytes move from central to peripheral plasmatic zone
  2. Rolling — selectins (E-, P-, L-selectin)
  3. Firm adhesion — integrins binding ICAM-1, VCAM-1
  4. Intraluminal crawling — slow migration
  5. Transmigration — paracellular (via PECAM-1, JAMs, VE-cadherin) or transcellular
  6. Basement membrane & interstitial migration — guided by chemokines (IL-8, C5a, LTB4)

  • Plasma protein systems: kinins (bradykinin), complement, coagulation/fibrinolysis
  • Arachidonic acid metabolites: prostaglandins, leukotrienes, PAF
  • Cytokines: IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α
  • Phagocyte products: ROS, cationic proteins, neutral proteases, NO

Resolution

  • Removal of stimulus
  • Apoptosis of neutrophils, efferocytosis by macrophages
  • Pro-resolving lipid mediators (lipoxins, resolvins, protectins)
  • Tissue returns to homeostasis

Chronicity

  • Persistent biofilm or antigen
  • Sustained macrophage/lymphocyte infiltrate
  • Tissue destruction, fibrosis, bone resorption
  • Imbalance of MMPs/TIMPs and RANKL/OPG

10. Relevance to Periodontal Disease

The periodontium is in constant contact with the subgingival biofilm. Host defense involves both innate (junctional epithelium barrier, GCF, neutrophils, complement) and adaptive (B and T lymphocytes producing antibodies and cytokines) responses.

  • Gingivitis — early lesion dominated by neutrophils and macrophages; established lesion shifts to plasma cells and B cells.
  • Periodontitis — chronic Th1/Th17-skewed response; IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α and PGE2 drive connective tissue breakdown via MMPs.
  • Alveolar bone loss — RANKL produced by Th17 and B cells activates osteoclasts; OPG levels are reduced.
  • Defective neutrophil function (e.g., LAD, Chédiak–Higashi) produces aggressive periodontal destruction, underlining the protective role of innate immunity.
  • IgG2 is the dominant antibody response against periodontal pathogens such as Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans; secretory IgA in saliva limits bacterial adhesion.
Key concept: Periodontal tissue destruction results not directly from bacteria but largely from a dysregulated, chronic host inflammatory–immune response. Modulating this response is the rationale behind host-modulation therapy (e.g., sub-antimicrobial doxycycline).

CHAPTER 5

Defense Mechanisms of the Gingiva

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed. (2018), Chapter 16 — Defense Mechanisms of the Gingiva.

Gingival tissue occupies a unique mucosal interface, in continuous contact with a dense and diverse bacterial biofilm. To preserve homeostasis at this exposed border, the gingiva integrates several active defense systems: the epithelial barrier (sulcular and junctional epithelium), the gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), saliva, migrating leukocytes, and soluble effectors such as antimicrobial peptides and complement.

1. Gingival Crevicular Fluid (GCF)

Origin and nature

GCF (also called sulcular fluid) seeps from the gingival vascular plexus through the junctional and sulcular epithelium into the gingival sulcus. In clinically healthy gingiva it behaves as a transudate (protein < 3 g/dL, cells < 1000/µL); during inflammation it becomes a true inflammatory exudate (protein > 3 g/dL, cells > 1000/µL). In normal sulci its volume is minimal or undetectable.

Composition

Cellular elements

  • Bacteria from the biofilm
  • Desquamated epithelial cells
  • Leukocytes (predominantly PMNs)

Molecular elements

  • Electrolytes: Na+, K+, Ca2+ (Na/K ratio rises with inflammation)
  • Carbohydrates: hexosamine, hexuronic acid; glucose 3-4× higher than in serum
  • Proteins, immunoglobulins (IgG predominates), complement
  • Host- and bacteria-derived enzymes
  • Cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β), prostaglandin E2

Functions

  • Mechanical flushing of the sulcus, washing out non-adherent bacteria and debris.
  • Delivery of specific antibodies and complement to neutralize subgingival microbes.
  • Transport of PMNs from connective tissue into the sulcus.
  • Carrier of host-derived enzymes and inflammatory mediators that modulate tissue response.
  • Excretion route for systemically administered antibiotics (e.g. tetracyclines, metronidazole) — relevant for periodontal pharmacotherapy.

Collection methods

Paper strips

Absorbent strips inserted into the sulcus; the wetted area is revealed by ninhydrin staining and measured planimetrically.

Micropipettes / capillary tubes

Allow precise volumetric collection of small fluid samples for biochemical assays.

Periotron®

Electronic device: wetting of a paper strip modifies an electrical signal, providing a digital readout. Correlates well with ninhydrin staining in vitro.

Clinical significance — diagnostic markers

GCF is a non-invasive, easily sampled biological fluid and is therefore a leading source of candidate biomarkers for periodontal disease activity and resolution. GCF volume increases with the severity of inflammation and is influenced by several factors:

FactorEffect on GCF flow
Circadian rhythmGradual rise from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m.; falls overnight
Female sex hormones (pregnancy, ovulation, oral contraceptives)Increase flow via vascular permeability
Mechanical stimulation (mastication, vigorous brushing)Acute increase
SmokingTransient acute rise; chronic suppression of GCF and saliva
Periodontal therapy (post-surgery)Increased flow during healing

No consistent correlation has been demonstrated between total GCF protein concentration and gingivitis severity, pocket depth, or alveolar bone loss — specific molecular markers, not total protein, are diagnostically relevant.

2. Permeability of the Sulcular and Junctional Epithelium

The junctional epithelium (JE) is the principal seal between the gingiva and the tooth surface. Despite this barrier role it is remarkably permeable: substances such as albumin, endotoxin (LPS), thymidine, histamine, phenytoin and horseradish peroxidase have all been shown to cross it. Movement occurs largely through the wide intercellular spaces — an essentially paracellular route — rather than across cell membranes.

Structural defense features of JE

  • Rapid turnover (4-6 days) renews the barrier and sheds attached bacteria.
  • Attachment to the tooth via hemidesmosomes and an internal basal lamina.
  • Loose intercellular junctions that allow controlled outflow of GCF and inward passage of immune cells.
  • Acts as a conduit for PMN migration into the sulcus.

Sulcular epithelium

  • Non-keratinized, thin, also permeable to bacterial products.
  • Faces the biofilm directly and is the first site of antigen sampling.

3. Leukocytes of the Dento-gingival Region

Even in clinically healthy, non-mechanically irritated sulci, leukocytes — predominantly PMNs — are present. They emerge in small numbers from vessels in the connective tissue apical to the sulcus and migrate through the junctional epithelium, ultimately being expelled into the gingival crevice.

  • The majority are viable and retain full phagocytic capacity.
  • Their migration occurs independently of an increase in vascular permeability, indicating a baseline surveillance role.
  • The rate of PMN migration correlates positively with gingival inflammation, making it a sensitive index of gingivitis.

Trans-junctional PMN traffic is the main cellular defense preventing apical extension of the subgingival biofilm.

4. Saliva

Saliva continuously bathes the oral cavity, contributing to gingival defense through mechanical cleansing, acid buffering, antimicrobial activity and modulation of plaque maturation.

Antimicrobial components

  • Lysozyme — hydrolyses peptidoglycan; active against Gram-positive and Gram-negative species (e.g. Veillonella, A. actinomycetemcomitans).
  • Lactoperoxidase-thiocyanate system — bactericidal for Lactobacillus and Streptococcus strains; blocks lysine and glutamic acid uptake.
  • Myeloperoxidase (leukocyte-derived) — bactericidal for Actinobacillus; inhibits adhesion of Actinomyces to hydroxyapatite.
  • Lactoferrin — iron sequestration starves bacteria.
  • Defensins and histatins — small cationic antimicrobial peptides active against bacteria and fungi.
  • Secretory IgA (sIgA) — dominant salivary antibody; locally synthesized; blocks adhesion of oral streptococci to epithelial cells. IgG and IgM are present but less abundant (IgG dominates in GCF).

Buffering and mineralization

The bicarbonate/carbonic acid system is the principal salivary buffer, maintaining physiologic pH at the epithelial and tooth surfaces. Saliva also supplies Ca2+ and phosphate for enamel remineralization, and contains coagulation factors (VIII, IX, X, Hageman factor) that shorten clotting time and protect wounds from bacterial colonization.

Salivary leukocytes and enzymes

Saliva carries desquamated epithelial cells and leukocytes (mainly PMNs); their count rises in gingivitis. Several enzymes are elevated in periodontal disease: hyaluronidase, lipase, β-glucuronidase, chondroitin sulfatase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, collagenase.

Xerostomia (Sjögren syndrome, sialolithiasis, sarcoidosis, Mikulicz disease, radiotherapy, surgical gland removal) reduces flow and buffering, predisposing to gingival inflammation, rampant caries and cervical/root caries.

5. Antimicrobial Peptides

Defensins

Cationic peptides produced by epithelial cells and neutrophils. α-defensins (HNP1-3) come from PMN granules; β-defensins (hBD-1, -2, -3) are expressed by junctional and sulcular epithelium. They disrupt bacterial membranes and recruit immune cells.

Cathelicidins (LL-37)

Single human cathelicidin, secreted by neutrophils and epithelium. Broad-spectrum bactericidal activity, neutralizes LPS, and acts as a chemoattractant for monocytes and T cells. Deficiency (Kostmann syndrome, Papillon-Lefèvre) is linked to severe early periodontitis.

6. The Complement System

Complement components reach the sulcus via GCF. Activation occurs by the classical (antibody-antigen), lectin (mannose-binding) and alternative (microbial surface) pathways. Within gingival defense complement contributes by:

  • Opsonization via C3b, enhancing phagocytosis of subgingival bacteria.
  • Chemotaxis through C3a and C5a, recruiting PMNs across the JE.
  • Lysis of susceptible Gram-negative organisms by the membrane attack complex (C5b-9).
  • Amplification of inflammation — dysregulated complement (notably C5a-C5aR1 signalling) is implicated in periodontitis pathogenesis.

CHAPTER 6

Gingival Inflammation

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed. (2018), Chapter 17 — Gingival Inflammation.

Introduction: From Health to Disease

Gingival health is actively maintained by both innate and specific (adaptive) immunity. The innate response includes epithelial cells, non-specific intraepithelial cells, mucins, lysozyme, lactoferrin, lactoperoxidase, and antimicrobial peptides such as histatins, beta-defensins, and protease inhibitors.

Pathological changes in gingivitis are initiated by the host response to oral microorganisms attached to the tooth surface and within or near the gingival sulcus. These organisms synthesise destructive products that damage epithelial cells, connective tissue, and intercellular substances (collagen, ground substance, glycocalyx).

Key bacterial / microbial products

  • Collagenase — degrades collagen fibers
  • Hyaluronidase — breaks down ground substance
  • Protease — lyses host proteins
  • Chondroitin sulfatase — degrades GAGs
  • Endotoxins (LPS) — activate host cells

Widening of the spaces between junctional epithelial cells in early gingivitis allows bacteria and their products to reach the connective tissue. Microbial products activate monocytes and macrophages to release vasoactive mediators — PGE2, interferon, TNF, and IL-1. IL-1β also blocks fibroblast apoptosis, stabilising the gingival fibroblast population during inflammation.

Clinically, gingivitis is divided into Stage I initial, Stage II early, and Stage III established lesions, with Stage IV (advanced lesion) corresponding to periodontitis. Transitions between stages are continuous, without sharp boundaries.

Most clinically healthy gingival biopsies already contain inflammatory cells — mostly T lymphocytes with very few B cells or plasma cells. These cells do not cause tissue damage but represent the day-to-day host response to constant bacterial challenge. A continuous flow of neutrophils normally migrates from the gingival plexus through the junctional epithelium into the sulcus and oral cavity.

Stage I — The Initial Lesion 2–4 days

The earliest manifestations of gingival inflammation are vascular changes: capillary dilation and increased blood flow. Clinically the response is not evident. Microscopically, classic features of acute inflammation appear in the connective tissue beneath the junctional epithelium. Within one week — sometimes as early as 2 days after plaque begins to accumulate — capillaries and venules widen and neutrophils adhere to vessel walls.

Leukocytes, mainly PMNs, leave the capillaries by diapedesis and migrate into the connective tissue, junctional epithelium, and gingival sulcus. Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) exudate and extravascular proteins appear. These findings are not accompanied by visible tissue damage and do not form an infiltrate — their presence is not pathological per se.

Outcome depends on the host response. The initial lesion may heal rapidly with restoration of normal tissue, or progress to a chronic inflammatory lesion. If it persists, an infiltrate of macrophages and lymphoid cells appears within days.

Stage II — The Early Lesion 4–7 days

The early lesion evolves from the initial lesion within about a week of plaque accumulation, without a clear point of separation. Clinically it overlaps with early gingivitis.

Clinical features

  • Erythema from capillary proliferation and loop formation between rete pegs
  • Bleeding on probing may be present
  • Early development of rete pegs / epithelial ridges

Histology

  • Leukocytic infiltrate beneath JE: 75% T lymphocytes
  • Plus migrating neutrophils, macrophages, plasma cells, mast cells
  • JE heavily infiltrated by PMNs
  • Increased collagen destruction — mainly circular and dentogingival fibers

PMNs follow chemotactic stimuli from plaque components, cross the basal lamina, and reach the pocket area, where they phagocytose bacteria and release lysosomal contents. Fibroblasts show cytotoxic changes and reduced collagen synthesis. At the molecular level, collagen degradation is driven by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which remodel the extracellular matrix.

Stage III — The Established Lesion 2–3 weeks

With time the early lesion evolves into the established lesion, characterised by predominance of plasma cells and B lymphocytes and the formation of a small gingival pocket lined by epithelium.

In chronic gingivitis (2–3 weeks after plaque accumulation begins), blood vessels become thickened and congested, venous return is impaired, and blood flow slows. The resulting localised gingival anoxia imparts a bluish-violet hue over the erythematous gingiva. Erythrocyte extravasation and haemoglobin breakdown further deepen the discoloration of chronically inflamed gingiva. Clinically this corresponds to moderate-to-severe gingivitis.

Defining histologic features

  • Intense chronic inflammatory reaction
  • Plasma cells become the predominant inflammatory cell — found immediately under the JE, around vessels, and between collagen bundles
  • JE shows widened intercellular spaces filled with granular cellular debris and lysosomes from disrupted PMNs, lymphocytes, and monocytes
  • Lysosomal acid hydrolases destroy tissue components
  • Rete pegs penetrate into connective tissue; basal lamina destroyed focally
  • Collagen destroyed around the cellular infiltrate

Established lesions are of two types: some remain stable for months or years, while others become active and progress to destructive lesions. Importantly, established lesions appear to be reversible — the tissue events following periodontal therapy mirror, in reverse, those of gingivitis progression.

Stage IV — The Advanced Lesion Periodontitis

Extension of the lesion into the alveolar bone characterises the advanced lesion, also known as the phase of periodontal destruction.

Microscopically, gingival fibrosis is present together with widespread inflammatory and immunopathologic tissue damage. Plasma cells dominate the connective tissue, while neutrophils continue to dominate the junctional epithelium.

Gingivitis to periodontitis — a susceptibility issue

Progression from gingivitis to periodontitis occurs only in susceptible individuals. Sites with marked bleeding (gingival index 2) show 70% more attachment loss than sites that remain non-inflamed (gingival index 0). Longitudinal studies in well-maintained populations have shown that persistent gingivitis is a risk factor for attachment loss and tooth loss. However, whether periodontitis can arise without preceding gingivitis is still unknown.

CHAPTER 7

Clinical Features of Gingivitis

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed. (2018), Chapter 18 — Clinical Features of Gingivitis.

Accumulation of microbial biofilm on clean tooth surfaces triggers an inflammatory process in the gingival tissue that persists as long as plaque remains adjacent to the gingiva and resolves with meticulous biofilm removal. More than half of the adult US population shows gingival bleeding, and gingivitis remains the most prevalent periodontal condition. Recent evidence suggests that distinct biological phenotypes may influence its development.

Clinically, gingivitis is defined by any of the following: redness and sponginess of the gingival tissue, bleeding on provocation, contour changes, and the presence of plaque or calculus — without radiographic evidence of crestal bone loss.

Course and Duration

Acute

Sudden onset, short duration, often painful.

Subacute

A less severe phase of the acute form.

Recurrent

Reappears after treatment or spontaneous resolution.

Chronic most common

Slow onset, long-standing, painless unless complicated by acute/subacute exacerbations. A fluctuating disease — inflammation may persist or resolve, while previously normal areas become inflamed.

Description: Distribution and Site

  • Localized — limited to the gingiva of a single tooth or group of teeth.
  • Generalized — involves the gingiva of all teeth.
  • Marginal — affects the gingival margin and may extend to adjacent attached gingiva.
  • Papillary — involves the interdental papillae; papillae are involved more frequently than the margin and often show the earliest signs of gingivitis.
  • Diffuse — affects margin, attached gingiva, and papillae.

Combined descriptors: localized marginal, localized diffuse, localized papillary, generalized marginal, and generalized diffuse (in which the mucogingival junction may be obliterated — systemic cofactors should be evaluated).

Clinical Findings — Systematic Examination

A systematic assessment of the gingiva includes color, contour, consistency, surface texture, position, and the ease and severity of bleeding and pain.

Gingival Bleeding on Probing (BOP)

The two earliest signs preceding established gingivitis are increased gingival crevicular fluid flow and bleeding from the sulcus on gentle probing. BOP appears earlier than color change or other visual signs and is an objective sign, making it preferable to subjective color assessment for early diagnosis.

Absence of BOP is highly desirable — it implies a low risk of future clinical attachment loss and supports a favorable prognosis.

Causes of Gingival Bleeding

Local factors

  • Plaque-retentive anatomy, caries, frenum pulls
  • Malposed teeth, mouth breathing, overhanging restorations
  • Ill-fitting partial dentures, lack of attached gingiva, recession
  • Orthodontic appliances and retainers (increase plaque retention and BOP)

Mechanical / chemical

  • Brushing, toothpicks, food impaction, biting hard foods
  • Toothbrush trauma in aggressive brushing
  • Thermal burns (hot foods) and chemical irritants increase ease of bleeding
  • Spontaneous bleeding in NUG via epithelial ulceration of necrotic surface
In moderate to advanced periodontitis, BOP is regarded as a sign of active tissue destruction. Systemic causes of abnormal bleeding include vitamin C deficiency, platelet disorders, hypoprothrombinemia (vit. K deficiency), hemophilia, leukemia, uremia, multiple myeloma, hormone therapy, oral contraceptives, pregnancy, menstrual cycle, and diabetes. Anticonvulsants, calcium-channel blockers, and immunosuppressants induce gingival overgrowth that may secondarily bleed.

Changes in Color

Gingival color depends on vascular supply, epithelial thickness, keratinization, and epithelial pigments. The gingiva turns red when vascularity increases or keratinization decreases; it appears pale with reduced vascularity or fibrosis. Chronic inflammation produces a deep red or bluish-red hue from vascular proliferation and decreased keratinization. Color change starts at the papillae and margin, progressing to attached gingiva.

  • Metallic pigmentation: bismuth, arsenic, mercury, lead, silver. Amalgam "tattoo" produces localized blue-black areas.
  • Endogenous pigments: melanin (physiologic in pigmented ethnic groups; pathologic in Addison's disease, Peutz-Jeghers, Albright, von Recklinghausen), bilirubin (jaundice — yellowish mucosa), iron (hemochromatosis — blue-gray).
  • Exogenous: coal/metal dust, food colorants, tobacco (hyperkeratosis and increased melanin pigmentation).

Changes in Consistency, Texture, and Position

Both acute and chronic inflammation alter the normally firm, resilient consistency. Chronic gingivitis displays a balance of destructive (edematous) and reparative (fibrotic) changes; the dominant component determines clinical consistency. Loss of stippling is considered an early sign of gingivitis. Surfaces become smooth and shiny (edematous), or firm and nodular (fibrotic); atrophic gingivitis and chronic desquamative gingivitis also produce smooth surfaces.

Pseudopockets vs True Pockets

Pseudopocket (gingivitis)

Deepened sulcus caused by coronal swelling of the gingival margin. The junctional epithelium remains at the CEJ — no attachment loss.

True periodontal pocket

Deepening from apical migration of the junctional epithelium with attachment and bone loss.

Gingival Recession

Recession — apical displacement of the gingival margin exposing the root surface — increases with age and is more prevalent in men. Two key positions must be distinguished:

  • Actual position — level of the coronal limit of epithelial attachment on the tooth (determines true recession severity).
  • Apparent position — level of the crest of the gingival margin (what is visible).

Total recession = visible (apparent) recession + hidden recession concealed by the pocket wall.

Etiologic factors: faulty toothbrushing (gingival abrasion), tooth malposition, soft-tissue friction (gingival ablation), gingival inflammation, abnormal frenum attachment, iatrogenic factors. Occlusal trauma has been proposed historically but never demonstrated.

Clinical significance: exposed root surfaces are susceptible to caries; abrasion/erosion of exposed cementum exposes sensitive dentin; pulpal hyperemia may follow extensive root exposure; interproximal recession impairs hygiene and promotes plaque accumulation. The Miller classification (Classes I–IV) is used to grade recession defects and predict root-coverage outcomes.

Contour Changes — Historical Descriptors

  • Stillman's clefts — narrow, triangular, elongated recession that extends apically, exposing root cementum.
  • McCall's festoons — thickened, rolled, often inflamed band of marginal gingiva, typically seen at canines with recession near the mucogingival junction.

CHAPTER 8

Acute Gingival Infections

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed., 2018 — Chapter 20: Acute Gingival Infections.

Acute gingival infections are rapidly evolving inflammatory or ulcerative conditions of the gingiva that typically arise in a compromised host. They include necrotizing forms (NUG/NUP), viral infections (primary herpetic gingivostomatitis), inflammation around partially erupted teeth (pericoronitis), and localized purulent collections (gingival/periodontal abscesses). Prompt recognition and differentiation are critical because management strategies differ substantially.

1. Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (NUG)

Also known historically as Vincent's infection, trench mouth, or acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis (ANUG). It is a microbial lesion that occurs in a debilitated or immunocompromised host and is characterized by necrosis and desquamation of gingival tissues with possible systemic involvement.

Etiology — the fusospirochetal complex

NUG is associated with a mixed anaerobic flora. The constant flora includes Prevotella intermedia, Fusobacterium species, Treponema species (spirochetes), and Selenomonas. A heterogeneous variable flora is also present. Spirochetes invade vital connective tissue to depths of up to 300 µm — a hallmark microbiologic feature. Resolution of clinical signs after metronidazole therapy supports the etiologic role of anaerobes.

Host response is required. Inoculation of bacterial exudates alone does not produce NUG experimentally. Immunosuppression is an essentially necessary predisposing condition.

Predisposing factors

Local
  • Pre-existing gingivitis or periodontal pockets
  • Pericoronal opercula and deep pockets (anaerobic niches)
  • Tobacco smoking (established high risk factor)
  • Traumatic gingival lesions
Systemic
  • Psychological stress, depression, insomnia
  • Nutritional deficiency (vitamin C, B2)
  • Debilitating disease (leukemia, anemia, ulcerative colitis)
  • HIV/AIDS and other immunodeficiencies
  • Alcohol and drug abuse, chronic fatigue

Clinical features

  • Oral signs: "punched-out" crater-like ulcerations of interdental papillae, covered by a gray pseudomembrane demarcated by a linear erythema. Marginal gingiva is red, shiny, hemorrhagic when the pseudomembrane is removed.
  • Symptoms: Severe radiating pain, intensified by spicy/hot food and chewing; metallic taste; pasty hypersalivation; fetor oris (foul halitosis); spontaneous gingival bleeding.
  • Systemic: Regional lymphadenopathy, low-grade fever; in severe cases — high fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis, anorexia, malaise. Children show more severe systemic reactions.
  • Rare sequelae: Gangrenous stomatitis and noma (cancrum oris) in malnourished children in developing countries.
Key point: NUG involves the gingival and junctional epithelium but typically does not form periodontal pockets, because a healthy junctional epithelium is required for pocket deepening.

Diagnosis and differential

Diagnosis is clinical — pain, ulceration, and bleeding. Bacterial smear/culture is not decisive but may help exclude specific infections. Differentials include primary herpetic gingivostomatitis, chronic desquamative gingivitis, chronic destructive periodontitis, diphtheria, secondary stage syphilis (mucous patch), aphthous stomatitis, actinomycosis, and streptococcal stomatitis. Underlying leukemia or HIV must always be considered.

Treatment

  • Gentle debridement of pseudomembrane and superficial deposits with cotton pellet soaked in chlorhexidine or hydrogen peroxide.
  • Oral hygiene instruction; 0.12% chlorhexidine rinses twice daily.
  • Systemic metronidazole 250 mg ×3/day for 7 days if systemic signs, fever, lymphadenopathy, or immunocompromised host.
  • Analgesics, hydration, smoking cessation, nutritional support, stress management.
  • Definitive scaling, root planing, and corrective periodontal/gingival contouring after acute phase resolves.

2. Necrotizing Ulcerative Periodontitis (NUP)

NUP represents the extension of necrotizing disease beyond the gingiva, with loss of clinical attachment and alveolar bone. The bacterial flora is similar to NUG. NUP is strongly associated with severe immunosuppression, particularly HIV/AIDS with CD4 < 200/mm³, and may progress rapidly to bone sequestration. Treatment combines local debridement, metronidazole, and management of the underlying systemic condition.

3. Primary Herpetic Gingivostomatitis

Acute infection of the oral cavity caused by Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1). Most often in infants and children under 6 years (also adolescents and young adults). In most individuals the primary infection is subclinical; after recovery, virus persists latent in sensory ganglia and may reactivate (herpes labialis, herpetic stomatitis, herpetic whitlow, encephalitis).

Clinical features

  • Onset: Acute, with diffuse, erythematous, shiny involvement of the gingiva and adjacent mucosa.
  • Lesions: Discrete spherical gray vesicles on gingiva, labial/buccal mucosa, soft palate, pharynx, sublingual mucosa, and tongue. After ~24 h they rupture into painful small ulcers with red, raised halo-like margins and yellowish-gray depressed centers. Lesions may coalesce.
  • Symptoms: Generalized oral pain interfering with eating, drinking, and oral hygiene; irritability and refusal to feed in infants.
  • Systemic: Cervical lymphadenitis, fever (38–40.6 °C), malaise.
  • Course: Self-limiting in 7–14 days; heals without scarring.

Histopathology

Targets epithelial cells, producing characteristic multinucleated Tzanck cells with ballooning degeneration, acantholysis, and intraepithelial vesicles.

Treatment

Supportive: hydration, soft bland diet, topical anesthetics (e.g., viscous lidocaine), antipyretics, and gentle plaque control. Systemic acyclovir (15 mg/kg 5×/day) shortens duration if initiated within 72 h of onset, especially in immunocompromised patients. Antibiotics are not indicated. It is contagious — universal precautions required.

4. Pericoronitis

Inflammation of the gingiva (operculum) surrounding the crown of a partially erupted tooth, most commonly the mandibular third molar. May be acute, subacute, or chronic.

Clinical features

  • Red, edematous, suppurating, exquisitely tender operculum.
  • Radiating pain to ear, throat, and floor of the mouth.
  • Foul taste, inability to close the jaws, persistent trismus.
  • Cheek swelling at the mandibular angle, regional lymphadenitis.
  • Systemic: fever, leukocytosis, malaise.
Serious complications: Pericoronal abscess, peritonsillar abscess, facial cellulitis, and Ludwig's angina (bilateral submandibular/sublingual cellulitis threatening the airway). Spread to retropharyngeal space is a surgical emergency.

Treatment

  • Gentle irrigation under the operculum with warm saline or chlorhexidine to remove debris.
  • Antibiotics (amoxicillin or metronidazole) if systemic signs, cellulitis, or trismus.
  • Occlusal adjustment of opposing cusp if traumatizing the operculum.
  • Definitive therapy after the acute episode: operculectomy or extraction of the involved tooth.

5. Gingival and Periodontal Abscesses

Gingival abscess
  • Localized to the marginal gingiva or interdental papilla.
  • Caused by foreign body impaction (popcorn husk, bristle).
  • No prior periodontal pocket.
  • Treatment: drainage, removal of irritant, irrigation.
Periodontal abscess
  • Within an existing periodontal pocket, deep in supporting tissues.
  • Tooth is vital and tender to percussion; mobile.
  • Treatment: drainage through pocket or incision, SRP, systemic antibiotics if systemic signs; later, definitive periodontal therapy.
Differential with periapical abscess: The periapical abscess arises from a non-vital tooth (negative pulp test), pain on percussion, swelling over the apex, periapical radiolucency. The periodontal abscess shows a vital tooth, swelling at the gingival margin, and a pre-existing pocket on probing.

CHAPTER 9

Pathogenesis of Periodontal Disease

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed., 2018 — Chapter 7, "Periodontal Disease Pathogenesis." Histologic staging based on Page & Schroeder (1976); contemporary model from Page & Kornman (1997).

Overview: A Dual Aetiology

Periodontitis is a dysbiotic, biofilm-driven inflammatory disease in which tissue destruction is produced predominantly by the host, not directly by the bacteria. The subgingival biofilm initiates and perpetuates an immune-inflammatory response; the magnitude, quality and resolution of that response determine whether disease progresses.

Central paradox. The defensive immune-inflammatory machinery — designed to protect — is responsible for the majority of the connective-tissue breakdown and alveolar bone loss seen clinically. Poor plaque control alone does not guarantee periodontitis, and excellent hygiene does not always prevent it: host susceptibility is the principal determinant.

The Page & Kornman Pathogenesis Model

The classical contemporary flow chart links the microbial trigger to clinical signs through an obligatory host-response intermediary, with all stages modulated by patient-level risk factors.

Bacterial Virulence Factors

Although the host causes most damage, microbial molecules are the indispensable trigger and contribute some direct cytotoxicity.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)

Outer-membrane endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria; lipid A is the bioactive moiety. Recognised by the CD14 / TLR-4 / MD-2 complex, triggering NF-κB-driven cytokine release. Lipoteichoic acid from Gram-positive cells acts similarly but more weakly.

Proteases & Gingipains

P. gingivalis secretes cysteine-proteases (Arg- and Lys-gingipains) that degrade collagen, elastin, fibronectin, complement components and cytokines, and activate PAR receptors to drive further inflammation.

Fimbriae

Filamentous surface projections. P. gingivalis FimA signals through TLR-2 to induce IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-α; engagement of complement receptor CR3 paradoxically suppresses IL-12, weakening NK / CD8+ killing of infected epithelial cells.

Capsule, Leukotoxin, Invasion

Capsular polysaccharides resist phagocytosis. A. actinomycetemcomitans produces a leukotoxin that kills PMNs and monocytes, and invades and persists within epithelial cells. F. nucleatum bridges co-aggregation and intracellular invasion.

Metabolic by-products

Ammonia (NH3), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), butyric and propionic acids — directly cytotoxic; butyrate induces apoptosis of T cells, B cells, fibroblasts and gingival epithelium.

Bacterial & extracellular DNA

Unmethylated CpG bacterial DNA stimulates TLR-9. eDNA is a ubiquitous structural element of biofilms, contributing to adhesion, antimicrobial resistance and horizontal gene transfer — and is a possible therapeutic target.

Host Response — Innate Immunity

The innate arm is fixed, non-specific and immediate. It is orchestrated through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) on sentinel cells (macrophages, dendritic cells) that bind microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs).

  • Neutrophils — the key effector cell of periodontal defence. They phagocytose bacteria but also release lysosomal enzymes, MMP-8, MMP-9 and reactive oxygen species (ROS) extracellularly, producing collateral collagen depletion. Quantitative or functional neutrophil deficits (e.g., LAD, Chédiak-Higashi, cyclic neutropenia, Papillon–Lefèvre) cause severe early-onset periodontitis.
  • TLRs — TLR-2 (lipoproteins, LTA, FimA), TLR-4 (LPS), TLR-9 (bacterial DNA) trigger NF-κB / MAPK cytokine cascades.
  • Complement — opsonisation (C3b), chemotaxis (C5a) and membrane attack complex. P. gingivalis manipulates C5aR / TLR-2 crosstalk to subvert killing.
  • Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) — α- and β-defensins, LL-37, calprotectin from epithelium and PMNs.
"Hyper-responsive" phenotype. Monocytes/neutrophils from periodontitis patients release exaggerated quantities of IL-1β, TNF-α, PGE2 and ROS in response to LPS. This individual trait — partly genetic, partly primed by environment — explains much of the variation in clinical susceptibility.

Host Response — Adaptive Immunity

If the innate response cannot clear the challenge, antigen-specific adaptive immunity is recruited. The shift in lesion composition mirrors disease stage.

  • Th1 — IFN-γ; cell-mediated immunity, possibly protective in early lesions.
  • Th2 — IL-4, IL-5, IL-13; drives B-cell / plasma-cell expansion, associated with progressive lesions.
  • Th17 — IL-17, IL-22; potent neutrophil recruiter, strongly osteoclastogenic via RANKL — a major destructive subset in periodontitis.

Tissue Destruction Mechanisms

Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs)

Zinc-dependent endopeptidases that degrade extracellular matrix. In health, fibroblast-derived MMP-1 turns over collagen physiologically. In disease, neutrophil-derived MMP-8 (collagenase-2) and MMP-9 (gelatinase B) dominate; their transcription is upregulated by IL-1β and TNF-α. GCF MMP-8 correlates with severity and falls after successful therapy — the basis for chair-side aMMP-8 tests.

The RANK / RANKL / OPG Axis — the master switch for bone resorption

Key concept. Osteoclast activity is governed by the balance between RANKL and its decoy OPG. The RANKL : OPG ratio determines whether the periodontium loses or maintains bone.
  • RANK — surface receptor on pre-osteoclasts / osteoclasts.
  • RANKL — ligand, membrane-bound or soluble, expressed by osteoblasts, fibroblasts, mesenchymal stromal cells and activated T and B lymphocytes.
  • OPG — soluble decoy receptor secreted mainly by osteoblasts and stromal cells; binds RANKL and prevents osteoclastogenesis.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Released by hyper-activated PMNs during respiratory burst. ROS damage collagen, lipids and DNA, activate latent MMPs, and amplify NF-κB signalling. Periodontitis patients exhibit reduced systemic antioxidant capacity, contributing to the oxidative stress component of disease.

Risk Factors & Modifiers of Susceptibility

Diabetes mellitus

AGE-RAGE signalling primes monocytes for exaggerated TNF-α / IL-6 release, impairs PMN chemotaxis, increases RANKL:OPG ratio, and slows wound healing. Bidirectional with glycaemic control.

Smoking

Vasoconstriction (masking BoP), impaired PMN function, reduced antibody response, dysbiotic shift, and lower treatment response. Strongest modifiable risk factor.

Genetics

Composite IL-1 gene polymorphisms (IL-1A +4845, IL-1B +3954) associated with hyperinflammatory phenotype. Familial aggregation in aggressive forms.

Stress

Cortisol-mediated immunosuppression, altered behaviours (hygiene, smoking, diet), increased pro-inflammatory cytokine release.

Obesity & metabolic syndrome

Adipokines (leptin, TNF-α, IL-6) sustain low-grade systemic inflammation that amplifies the local periodontal response.

Acquired / other

Medications (immunosuppressants, calcium-channel blockers), pregnancy, osteoporosis, HIV — all alter immune balance or tissue response.

Pocket Formation — the Self-perpetuating Cycle

As inflammation deepens, coronal junctional-epithelium cells detach while the JE migrates apically along the root surface, attempting to maintain an intact barrier over collagen-depleted areas. The deepening pocket forms an anaerobic, warm, nutrient-rich niche favouring dysbiotic, periodontopathic species — bacteria that are effectively "outside" the body and beyond the reach of host clearance. Continued antigenic challenge → continued cytokine release → continued MMP activity and RANKL-driven bone loss → deeper pocket → worse plaque control. Resolution requires active pro-resolving signalling (lipoxins, resolvins, protectins) which is impaired in chronic disease.

Clinical take-home. Therapy must address both arms of the model: mechanical disruption of the dysbiotic biofilm and modification of host susceptibility (smoking cessation, glycaemic control, weight, stress). Host-modulation agents (sub-antimicrobial doxycycline, resolvins, anti-cytokine biologics) target the destructive cascade itself.

CHAPTER 10

Bone Loss & Patterns of Bone Resorption

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed. (2018), Chapter 24 — Bone Loss and Patterns of Bone Destruction.

Bone loss is the defining histopathologic event that converts gingivitis into periodontitis. It is the cumulative result of inflammatory mediator-driven osteoclast activation extending from the gingival margin into the supporting alveolar bone. The pattern of resorption (horizontal vs. vertical, defect wall count, furcation involvement) directly dictates prognosis and the feasibility of regenerative therapy.

1. Mechanisms of Bone Destruction

Bone loss in periodontitis represents the final stage of the inflammatory cascade. Resorption is mediated by both bacterial and host factors acting at the bone surface.

Bacterial Contribution

  • Biofilm metabolites induce differentiation of precursor cells into osteoclasts.
  • LPS and other PAMPs stimulate gingival cells to release osteolytic mediators.
  • Direct inhibition of osteoblast activity / reduction in osteoblast numbers.

Host Mediators

  • Prostaglandins (PGE2) and their precursors.
  • IL-1α, IL-1β
  • TNF-α
  • RANK / RANKL / OPG axis (see Ch 9): RANKL on activated T-cells & osteoblasts binds RANK on osteoclast precursors driving osteoclastogenesis; OPG acts as a decoy receptor whose decline tilts the balance toward resorption.

Pathway of Extension

  • Interproximal: inflammation spreads through loose connective tissue around blood vessels, then through vascular channels piercing the crest of the interdental septum into marrow spaces.
  • Facial / lingual: spread along external periosteum, penetrating marrow via cortical vascular channels.
  • Marrow is replaced by leukocytic exudate, neovasculature, and fibroblasts; Howship's lacunae line resorbed surfaces.

Radius of action: Local factors must lie within 1.5–2.5 mm of the bone surface to cause resorption. Interproximal angular defects can only form when the interdental space exceeds 2.5 mm — narrower spaces yield horizontal loss. Average untreated progression: 0.2–0.3 mm/year, episodic (bursts of activity alternating with quiescence).

3. Classification of Infrabony Defects (Goldman & Cohen)

Angular defects are classified by the number of residual osseous walls bordering the defect. Wall count predicts containment of graft material and regenerative outcome.

3-Wall Defect Best

Bounded by tooth on one side and three osseous walls (mesial, distal, lingual or facial). Best containment, highest regenerative success — graft + barrier membrane indicated.

2-Wall Defect Intermediate

Most common 2-wall = interdental crater (facial & lingual walls remain, proximal bone gone). Moderate regenerative response.

1-Wall Defect Worst

Single wall remains — often a proximal hemiseptum. Poor containment, worst regenerative prognosis; resective therapy frequently chosen.

Combination & Circumferential (4-wall) Defects

Combination defect: the apical portion has more walls than the coronal portion (e.g., 3-wall apically, 2-wall coronally). Circumferential / 4-wall defect: a continuous trough-like defect involving more than one surface of the tooth, fully encircling the root.

5. Other Osseous Defects & Architectural Variants

Osseous Craters

Specific 2-wall defect: concavity in the crest of interproximal bone bounded by facial and lingual walls. Among the most prevalent infrabony defects.

Bulbous Bone Contours

Bony enlargements from exostoses, functional adaptation, or buttressing bone formation. More common in the maxilla.

Ledges

Plateau-like bony margins produced by resorption of thickened cortical plates.

Reversed (Negative) Architecture

Loss of interproximal bone without equivalent loss of radicular bone — inverting the normal scalloped contour. More frequent in maxilla.

Dehiscence

Cleft-like resorption extending from the alveolar crest apically along the root, exposing the root surface (no crestal bone covering coronal portion).

Fenestration

Isolated window of root exposure where the crestal bone remains intact but a defect exists below it.

6. Radiographic Features & Limitations

  • Radiographs show two-dimensional bone level — facial and lingual plates are superimposed on the root; defects on these surfaces are often invisible.
  • Bone loss is typically detectable only after 30–50% mineral loss, so true loss exceeds radiographic appearance.
  • Furcation radiolucencies (furcation arrow) and loss of crestal cortication are key early signs.
  • Buccal/lingual craters, dehiscences, and fenestrations are not reliably visualized — clinical probing and CBCT supplement.

7. Modifying Factors & Prognostic Considerations

Anatomic Determinants of Pattern

  • Thickness, width & crestal angulation of interdental septa
  • Thickness of facial/lingual cortical plates
  • Pre-existing fenestrations & dehiscences
  • Tooth alignment, root anatomy, root trunk length, root proximity
  • Thin radicular bone ⇒ cannot support angular defects; produces horizontal loss

Occlusal Trauma & Systemic

  • Pure occlusal trauma: reversible PDL widening, funnel-shaped crest — no attachment loss.
  • Trauma + inflammation: accelerated bone loss, favors vertical/atypical patterns.
  • Systemic: osteoporosis, hyperparathyroidism, leukemia, histiocytosis X aggravate bone loss.
  • Buttressing bone (central or peripheral) attempts to reinforce weakened trabeculae.

Therapeutic key: 3-wall and deep narrow infrabony defects respond best to regenerative therapy (GTR, bone graft, biologics). 1-wall and broad craters generally require resective osseous surgery to re-establish positive architecture. The primary goal of periodontal therapy remains elimination of inflammation so that reparative tendencies — including bone formation occurring even during active disease — can predominate.

Q1

Differentiate horizontal and vertical (angular) bone loss.

A1

Horizontal: bone resorbs parallel to a line connecting CEJs of adjacent teeth; most common pattern; produces suprabony pockets. Vertical/angular: oblique resorption creating an infrabony defect with the base of pocket apical to alveolar crest.

Q2

Define infrabony defect and how it's classified by number of walls.

A2

Defect where pocket base is apical to alveolar crest. 1-wall (worst prognosis), 2-wall (interproximal "crater" most common), 3-wall (intrabony, best regenerative prognosis — root + 3 bony walls), and combined defects.

Q3

Which infrabony defect has the best regenerative outcome and why?

A3

The 3-wall (intrabony) defect: surrounded on 3 sides by bone with the root as the 4th, providing maximum source of osteogenic/PDL cells and good space maintenance for graft/membrane. Narrow defect angle (<25°) also favors regeneration.

Q4

List Glickman's classification of furcation involvement.

A4

Grade I: incipient, pocket into flute, no radiographic change. Grade II: cul-de-sac, partial penetration, bone remains. Grade III: through-and-through but covered by gingiva. Grade IV: through-and-through, clinically visible (gingival recession).

Q5

List Hamp's horizontal furcation classification.

A5

Class I: horizontal probing <3 mm into furcation. Class II: ≥3 mm but not through. Class III: through-and-through. Based on horizontal probing depth with Nabers probe; uses millimeter cutoffs unlike Glickman.

Q6

Which instrument is used to assess furcation involvement and how?

A6

Nabers probe (curved, color-coded). Inserted horizontally into the furcation entrance; depth and width determine the class. Each multi-rooted tooth has different access points (maxillary molars: B, MP, DP; mandibular: B, L).

Q7

Compare suprabony and infrabony pockets.

A7

Suprabony: base of pocket coronal to crestal bone; horizontal bone loss; transseptal fibers reorganize horizontally. Infrabony: base of pocket apical to crestal bone; vertical bone loss; transseptal fibers reorganize obliquely.

Q8

What is a dehiscence vs a fenestration?

A8

Dehiscence: V-shaped defect extending from alveolar crest apically, exposing root. Fenestration: isolated window in bone exposing root surface with intact crestal margin. Common in prominent roots or thin biotype.

Q9

What is a craters and what is its prevalence?

A9

An interproximal 2-wall infrabony defect with buccal and lingual walls intact but interdental bone resorbed. Most common infrabony defect (~35% of all defects, ~60% of mandibular defects).

Q10

What radiographic features suggest active bone loss vs healed bone?

A10

Active: fuzzy, ill-defined crestal cortication, "moth-eaten" appearance. Healed: dense, well-corticated crest. Loss of crestal lamina dura is the earliest radiographic sign. Radiographs underestimate bone loss by ~1-1.5 mm.

Q11

Describe Goldman & Cohen's classification of bone defects.

A11

Suprabony (horizontal) and infrabony defects. Infrabony subdivided into intrabony (1-, 2-, 3-wall, combined — defect within bone surrounding root) and interradicular (furcation defects).

Q12

How does the angle of an infrabony defect influence treatment outcome?

A12

Narrow defects (angle <25°) respond better to regenerative therapy than wide defects (>37°). Deeper defects (≥3 mm) and narrow angles favor space maintenance and clot stabilization for new attachment.

Smoking & Periodontal Disease

Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed., 2018 - Chapter 12: Smoking and Periodontal Disease

Epidemiology & Public Health Burden

Smoking is the most significant modifiable risk factor for periodontitis. Roughly one third of adult males and under 10% of adult females smoke regularly, with the highest prevalence in developing countries. Tobacco smoke contains benzene, nicotine, tar, formaldehyde, and numerous other carcinogens; smokers lose up to 20 years of life expectancy to lung cancer, cardiovascular and respiratory disease.

Clinical headline: Nearly half of all periodontitis cases diagnosed in the United States list smoking as the principal risk factor. Every patient must be questioned about smoking history, type, and frequency at intake and at every recall.

Dose-Response Relationship

  • Smokers are ~2x more likely to develop periodontitis than non-smokers.
  • Risk rises exponentially with cigarettes per day and with cumulative pack-years.
  • Older long-term smokers carry up to 3x the risk of severe periodontitis.
  • Young smokers are nearly 4x more likely to develop aggressive forms.
  • Smoking accelerates tissue destruction even when plaque levels are low.

Effects on Periodontal Tissues

Gingiva
  • Clinical inflammation appears less evident than the actual disease severity
  • Pale, fibrotic appearance
  • Reduced bleeding on probing for an equivalent plaque load
Deeper Periodontium
  • Greater clinical attachment loss
  • Greater alveolar bone loss
  • More tooth loss
  • Deeper pockets, particularly on anterior and palatal surfaces
  • More furcation involvement in molars
  • More gingival recession - the classic "smoker's pattern" (anterior maxillary palatal recession and staining)

Effects on Microbiology

Subgingival biofilm composition differs in smokers, favouring colonisation by aggressive periodontal pathogens and persistence after therapy.

  • Higher counts of red-complex pathogens: Tannerella forsythia, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Treponema denticola.
  • Higher counts of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans.
  • Increased orange-complex species (Eikenella nodatum, Fusobacterium nucleatum ss. vincentii, P. intermedia, Prevotella nigrescens) - precursors that prepare the niche for red-complex colonisation.
  • Pathogens colonise shallow pockets in smokers, not just deep ones.
  • Greater persistence/regrowth after mechanical debridement.

Effects on Host Response

Cellular defence
  • Impaired neutrophil chemotaxis
  • Reduced phagocytosis
  • Decreased hypochlorite production (oxidative burst)
Humoral defence
  • Decreased serum IgG2 response, particularly against A. actinomycetemcomitans
  • Loss of protective antibody coverage against periopathogens
Cytokine / mediator profile
  • Nicotine-stimulated monocytes overproduce PGE2 in response to LPS
  • Elevated TNF-alpha and overall pro-inflammatory load
  • Increased MMP-8 in gingival crevicular fluid - more collagen breakdown
  • Paradoxically reduced IL-1 expression in some studies, but the net effect is heightened oxidative stress
Vascular physiology
  • Nicotine-induced vasoconstriction of gingival microcirculation
  • Reduced subgingival O2 tension and temperature
  • Pale gingiva even in health
  • Impaired wound healing
Masked bleeding on probing. Vasoconstriction suppresses the classic BoP sign. The gingiva can look deceptively healthy while attachment loss continues silently in deep tissues. Never use BoP alone to gauge disease activity in a smoker - rely on probing depth, CAL, radiographs, and plaque control.

Why is smoking considered the most important modifiable risk factor for periodontitis?

How does smoking alter the subgingival microbiota?

Increased prevalence of periodontal pathogens (T. forsythia, P. gingivalis, T. denticola, A. actinomycetemcomitans) in shallower pockets; reduced oxygen tension favoring anaerobes; earlier dysbiosis.

How does smoking affect response to non-surgical periodontal therapy?

Smokers show less PD reduction (~0.5 mm less), less CAL gain, and higher recurrence rates after SRP. Effects are dose-dependent. Smokers benefit but should be informed of attenuated response.

What is the impact of smoking on regenerative and implant therapy?

Regenerative procedures (GTR, bone grafts): reduced CAL gain, less bone fill. Implants: higher peri-implantitis and failure rates (~2-3x), especially in heavy smokers; relative contraindication.

What is smoker's melanosis?

Diffuse brown-black pigmentation of attached gingiva (anterior) and buccal mucosa due to stimulation of melanocytes by tobacco. Reversible after smoking cessation. Benign but should be distinguished from other pigmented lesions.

What effect does smoking have on PMN function?

Reduced chemotaxis and phagocytosis but increased ROS production and proteolytic enzyme release — a "frustrated" phenotype that fails to clear bacteria while damaging host tissues. Also reduced antibody (IgG2) titers against pathogens.

How does smoking cessation affect periodontal risk?

A10

Risk decreases progressively: ex-smokers show better treatment response than current smokers within 1 year; after ~11 years, risk approaches non-smoker levels. Cessation counseling is part of Step 1 EFP therapy.

Q11

What is the "5A" smoking cessation framework?

A11

Ask (about tobacco use), Advise (to quit), Assess (willingness), Assist (counseling, pharmacotherapy: NRT, varenicline, bupropion), Arrange (follow-up). Brief intervention by dental professional is evidence-based and effective.

Q12

A12

No. Smokeless tobacco causes localized recession and leukoplakia at placement site. E-cigarettes show altered microbiome, increased inflammation, and emerging evidence of attachment loss — not a safe alternative.

Periodontal Examination — The Core

Probing Depth (PD)

PD is the distance from the free gingival margin to the base of the sulcus/pocket, measured with a calibrated periodontal probe.

UNC-15

15 mm probe with millimetre markings at every mm and colour bands at 4-5 and 9-10, 14-15. Universal research/clinical probe.

WHO/CPITN 621

Ball-tipped (0.5 mm) probe with black band 3.5-5.5 mm. Used for PSR/BPE screening.

Williams probe

Markings at 1-2-3-5-7-8-9-10 mm. Classic clinical probe.
Technique: Probe held parallel to long axis of tooth, walked in 1 mm steps around each surface, with a controlled force of 0.20-0.25 N (the weight that just blanches the thumbnail). Six sites per tooth: MB, B, DB, ML, L, DL. Read to the nearest whole mm (round up).

Clinical Attachment Level (CAL)

CAL is the distance from the cement-enamel junction (CEJ) to the base of the pocket. It is the gold standard for measuring true periodontal destruction because it is independent of gingival margin position.

Calculation:
• If GM is apical to CEJ (recession): CAL = PD + recession
• If GM is coronal to CEJ (oedema/overgrowth): CAL = PD – (distance GM to CEJ)
• If GM is at the CEJ: CAL = PD
Why CAL > PD for staging: A 5 mm pocket with 3 mm recession represents 8 mm of attachment loss (severe). A 5 mm pseudopocket from gingival overgrowth with GM 2 mm coronal to CEJ represents only 3 mm CAL (mild). The 2017 classification stages periodontitis on interdental CAL, not PD.

Gingival Margin Position & Recession

Record the position of the gingival margin relative to the CEJ (positive value = recession; negative = coronal to CEJ). Miller's classification (I-IV) further describes recession defects by relation to mucogingival junction and interdental bone.

Bleeding on Probing (BoP)

  • Recorded as present/absent at each of the six sites, 10-30 seconds after probing.
  • BoP% = (bleeding sites / total sites probed) × 100.
  • Full-mouth BoP <10% is the threshold for periodontal stability.
  • Absence of BoP has high negative predictive value for disease progression (~98%); presence has lower positive predictive value.

Suppuration

Pus expressed on digital pressure or probing indicates active infection with pocket epithelial ulceration. Always recorded; combined with BoP it suggests an unstable site.

Furcation Involvement

Assessed with a curved Nabers probe at every multi-rooted tooth: buccal & lingual of mandibular molars; buccal, mesiopalatal, distopalatal of maxillary molars.

Bleeding within 30 seconds of probing with light force. Health: <10% sites. Localized gingivitis: 10-30%. Generalized gingivitis: >30%. Absence of BOP is strong negative predictor (>90%) of disease progression.

4 sites/tooth scored 0-3: 0 = no plaque; 1 = plaque only on probe; 2 = visible plaque; 3 = abundant plaque. Average per tooth and per mouth. Assesses plaque at gingival margin (etiologic significance).

Grade 0: physiologic. Grade 1: horizontal mobility <1 mm. Grade 2: horizontal mobility >1 mm. Grade 3: severe horizontal AND vertical (depressibility) mobility. Tested with two rigid instruments, not fingers.

Q8

What is the Gingival Index (Löe & Silness 1963)?

A8

4 sites/tooth scored 0-3: 0 = normal; 1 = mild inflammation, no BOP; 2 = redness/edema with BOP; 3 = severe inflammation with spontaneous bleeding. Measures gingival inflammation severity.

Q9

What is the PSR (Periodontal Screening and Recording) index?

A9

Mouth divided into 6 sextants; WHO probe used; highest code recorded per sextant. Code 0-4: 0 = healthy, 1 = BOP only, 2 = calculus/overhangs, 3 = PD 4-5 mm, 4 = PD ≥6 mm. Code 3/4 requires full charting.

Q10

What radiographic projections are used in periodontal diagnosis?

A10

Full-mouth periapicals with paralleling technique (gold standard for bone level); vertical bitewings for posteriors (best for early bone loss); panoramic for screening; CBCT for furcation and infrabony defects when 2D inadequate.

Q11

What is the normal CEJ-to-alveolar-crest distance and what indicates bone loss?

A11

Normal: 1-2 mm (≤2 mm). >2 mm suggests bone loss. Radiographic bone loss underestimates true loss by 1-1.5 mm. Earliest sign: loss of crestal cortication (lamina dura).

Q12

What information does a complete periodontal chart include?

A12

PD, CAL, gingival margin position, BOP, suppuration, mobility, furcation involvement, plaque scores, mucogingival defects, keratinized tissue width, occlusal contacts/trauma, restorations, missing teeth, and full-mouth radiographs.

Q13

How is "% bone loss/age" calculated for grading?

A13

Worst-site radiographic bone loss (% of root length) divided by patient age. <0.25 = Grade A, 0.25-1.0 = B, >1.0 = C. Example: 30% bone loss in a 60-year-old = 0.5 → Grade B.

Q14

List the criteria used to assess prognosis of an individual tooth.

A14

Probing depth and CAL, mobility, furcation involvement, crown-to-root ratio, root morphology, restorability, endodontic status, patient compliance, smoking, systemic conditions, strategic value. McGuire & Nunn or Kwok & Caton systems.

CHAPTER 13

Periodontal Treatment

Reference: Carranza's Clinical Periodontology, 13th ed., Sections VI-IX (Treatment of Periodontal Disease); Newman & Carranza; EFP S3 Clinical Practice Guideline (Sanz M. et al., J Clin Periodontol 2020); AAP Best Evidence Reviews.

Goals of Periodontal Therapy

Overarching therapeutic objectives:
  • Arrest disease progression — eliminate the bacterial challenge and stop further attachment/bone loss.
  • Reduce inflammation — resolve bleeding on probing (BoP), suppuration, and clinical signs of disease.
  • Restore function and aesthetics — re-establish a healthy, maintainable, functional dentition with acceptable appearance.
  • Prevent recurrence — long-term stability through supportive periodontal therapy (SPT) and risk-factor control.
  • Ideal endpoint: shallow pockets (≤4 mm) without BoP, no plaque-retentive factors, plaque score <20%, stable clinical attachment level (CAL).

EFP S3 Clinical Practice Guideline — The 4-Step Treatment Framework

The European Federation of Periodontology S3-level Clinical Practice Guideline (2020) defines a stepwise, evidence-based approach for the treatment of Stage I-III periodontitis. Each step is performed sequentially, and progression to the next step depends on the response to the previous one. This is the contemporary international standard and the exam framework.

Step 1 — Behavioural Change and Supragingival Biofilm Control

Oral Hygiene Instruction (OHI)
  • Modified Bass technique; powered brushes (oscillating-rotating, sonic) have small but consistent benefit
  • Interdental cleaning: interdental brushes preferred over floss when embrasures allow
  • Tongue cleaning, chemical adjuncts (essential oils, CHX short-term)
  • Disclosing solutions to demonstrate plaque
  • Motivational interviewing > lecture-style instruction
Risk Factor Control
  • Smoking cessation — the single most impactful intervention; brief advice, NRT, behavioural support
  • Diabetes — HbA1c <7% targets; bi-directional referral with physician
  • Stress reduction, diet, weight management
  • Removal of plaque-retentive factors: overhangs, ill-fitting prostheses, caries
  • Professional Mechanical Plaque Removal (PMPR) supragingivally

Step 2 — Scaling and Root Planing (SRP)

Scaling is the removal of plaque and calculus from supragingival and subgingival tooth surfaces. Root planing is the additional smoothing of the cementum to remove embedded calculus and the endotoxin (LPS)-contaminated superficial layer. Together they constitute subgingival instrumentation, the cornerstone of cause-related therapy.

Step 3 — Periodontal Surgery

General Indications & Contraindications

Indications
  • Residual PD ≥5 mm with BoP after Step 2
  • Deep PD ≥6 mm regardless of BoP
  • Infrabony defects amenable to regeneration
  • Furcation involvement (Class II)
  • Mucogingival deformities, recession defects
  • Inadequate access for non-surgical instrumentation
  • Drug-induced gingival enlargement persisting after Phase I
Contraindications
  • Poor plaque control / non-compliant patient
  • Uncontrolled systemic disease (diabetes, bleeding disorders)
  • High caries risk, untreated caries
  • Heavy smoking (relative; outcomes worse)
  • Unrealistic patient expectations
  • Hopeless tooth prognosis

Access Flap — Modified Widman Flap (MWF)

Indications: moderate-to-severe pockets (5-7 mm), preparation for regenerative procedures, when bone architecture preservation is needed. Goal: access for debridement and primary closure, NOT pocket elimination.

Resective Surgery

  • Gingivectomy / Gingivoplasty (Goldman): external bevel incision apical to pocket base; for suprabony pockets, drug-induced enlargement, pseudopockets, idiopathic fibromatosis. Heals by secondary intention. Contraindicated when bone surgery needed or attached gingiva inadequate.
  • Apically positioned flap: pocket elimination with preservation of keratinized tissue.
  • Osseous resective surgery — reshape bone to "positive architecture":
    • Ostectomy — removal of supporting bone (in contact with root)
    • Osteoplasty — reshaping of non-supporting bone (no root contact)
  • Distal wedge / proximal wedge: tuberosity and retromolar pocket elimination.

Regenerative Procedures

  • Healing by "bridging" + "creeping attachment"

  • Subepithelial CT harvested from palate (Langer, Bruno techniques)

  • High frenal attachment contributing to recession
  • Shallow vestibule limiting hygiene
  • Often combined with FGG

Crown Lengthening

Biologic width (supracrestal tissue attachment): minimum 3 mm from restoration margin to alveolar crest = 1 mm sulcus + 1 mm junctional epithelium + 1 mm connective tissue attachment (Gargiulo 1961). Violation causes chronic inflammation, bone loss, recession.
  • Indications: subgingival caries/fractures, short clinical crowns, restorative access, aesthetic gummy smile (with altered passive eruption)
  • Surgical technique: apically positioned flap + ostectomy to establish 3 mm from future margin to crest
  • Wait 6 weeks (anterior aesthetic: 6 months) before final restoration
  • Alternative: orthodontic forced eruption ("rapid extrusion")

Occlusal Therapy

  • Selective grinding / coronoplasty — eliminate premature contacts, balancing interferences in patients with occlusal trauma + mobility
  • Splinting — indicated only when mobility increases progressively or impairs function/comfort; not for routine periodontitis
  • Nightguards / occlusal splints — for bruxism, parafunction
  • Note: occlusal trauma is a cofactor and does not initiate periodontitis (Lindhe-Glickman debate resolved)

Implant Therapy in the Periodontal Patient

Periodontitis patients have a higher risk of peri-implantitis (OR ~2-5x). Implants should be placed only after periodontal stability is achieved and within an SPT program.
  • Treat active periodontitis FIRST — do not place implants in untreated mouths
  • Reduce all PD ≤4 mm before implant placement
  • Strict SPT every 3-4 months for peri-implant health
  • Smoking cessation strongly recommended pre-implant

Step 4 — Supportive Periodontal Therapy (SPT) / Maintenance

Lifelong phase — the determinant of long-term outcome. Axelsson & Lindhe (1981, 30-year follow-up) and Nyman et al. demonstrated that even advanced periodontitis can be stabilized for decades with rigorous SPT, whereas patients without SPT lose attachment at rates comparable to untreated disease.

"Treat-to-target" endpoints (EFP):
  • Probing depth ≤4 mm at all sites
  • No bleeding on probing at sites with PD ≥4 mm
  • Full-mouth plaque score <20% (ideally <15%)
  • Full-mouth BoP <10%
  • Stable CAL over time
  • No suppuration, mobility controlled, function and aesthetics acceptable to patient

Q1

List the 4 steps of the EFP S3 treatment guideline.

A1

Step 1: behavioral change, OHI, risk-factor control, supragingival biofilm/calculus removal. Step 2: subgingival instrumentation (SRP) ± adjuncts. Step 3: surgical therapy for residual pockets. Step 4: Supportive periodontal therapy (SPT/maintenance).

Q2

What is the goal/endpoint of therapy used to decide on Step 3?

A2

Treatment endpoint = no PD >4 mm with BOP and no PD ≥6 mm. After Step 2 reassessment (6-12 weeks), sites failing to meet endpoint may require surgical (Step 3) intervention. PD 4 mm without BOP = stable.

Q3

Differentiate scaling and root planing (SRP).

A3

Scaling: removal of plaque and calculus from supra- and subgingival surfaces. Root planing: removal of softened/contaminated cementum and smoothing of the root surface. Together = subgingival instrumentation. Modern concept favors thorough debridement without aggressive cementum removal.

Q4

Compare full-mouth disinfection (FMD) and quadrant SRP.

A4

FMD (Quirynen): full-mouth SRP within 24 h + chlorhexidine on all niches to prevent recolonization. Quadrant: 4 sessions 1-2 weeks apart. Both have similar clinical outcomes per Cochrane; choice based on patient/logistics.

Q5

Name common adjuncts to SRP and indications.

A5

Local antimicrobials (chlorhexidine chip, minocycline microspheres, doxycycline gel) for residual pockets; systemic antibiotics (amoxicillin 500 mg + metronidazole 400 mg TID 7 days) for Stage III/IV Grade C in younger patients; SDD (host modulation).

Q6

List indications for periodontal surgery (Step 3).

A6

Residual PD ≥6 mm or PD ≥5 mm with BOP after Step 2; need for access to deep/complex defects; regeneration of intrabony or Class II furcation defects; resective therapy of suprabony pockets; mucogingival corrections; crown lengthening.

Q7

Differentiate resective vs regenerative periodontal surgery.

A7

Resective (osseous surgery, apically positioned flap, gingivectomy): eliminates pocket by reshaping bone/soft tissue; healing by long JE. Regenerative (GTR, bone grafts, EMD): aims to restore lost attachment apparatus (cementum, PDL, bone).

Q8

What is guided tissue regeneration (GTR) and its principle?

A8

Use of barrier membrane (resorbable: collagen/polylactic; non-resorbable: ePTFE) to exclude gingival epithelium and connective tissue from the wound, allowing slower PDL and bone cells to repopulate the root → true regeneration with new cementum, PDL, and bone.

Q9

List types of bone grafts used in periodontal regeneration.

A9

Autograft (patient's own — gold standard, osteogenic), allograft (DFDBA/FDBA from donor — osteoinductive/osteoconductive), xenograft (bovine — osteoconductive), alloplast (synthetic: HA, β-TCP, bioactive glass — osteoconductive scaffold).

Q10

What is enamel matrix derivative (EMD/Emdogain) and its mechanism?

A10

Porcine-derived amelogenin proteins that mimic Hertwig's epithelial root sheath signaling; promote acellular cementum formation, PDL fibers, and bone — true regeneration in intrabony defects. Applied to conditioned root after debridement.

Q11

What flap designs are used in modern regenerative periodontal surgery?

A11

Papilla preservation flaps: simplified papilla preservation flap (Cortellini, narrow papillae <2 mm), modified papilla preservation technique (wide papillae ≥2 mm), and minimally invasive surgical technique (MIST/M-MIST) — preserve blood supply and clot stability.

Q12

What is supportive periodontal therapy (SPT) and standard recall interval?

A12

Lifelong maintenance after active therapy: reassessment, OHI reinforcement, professional debridement of residual sites, risk-factor monitoring. Standard interval 3-4 months; based on patient risk (Lang & Tonetti spider diagram). Critical to prevent recurrence.

Q13

List Lang & Tonetti's risk factors used to determine SPT interval.

A13

% BOP, number of PD ≥5 mm, number of teeth lost, bone loss/age, systemic/genetic factors, smoking status. Plotted on a spider diagram giving low/medium/high risk → SPT every 12 / 6 / 3 months respectively.

Q14

What types of healing are possible after periodontal therapy?

A14

Repair (long junctional epithelium — most common after SRP/flap); reattachment (reunion of pre-existing fibers after surgical separation); new attachment/regeneration (new cementum, PDL, bone — only with GTR, EMD, or biologics); ankylosis (pathologic).
